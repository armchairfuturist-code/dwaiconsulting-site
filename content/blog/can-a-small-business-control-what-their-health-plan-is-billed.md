#### How Reference-Based Pricing Can Help Small Employers Control Health Insurance Costs

Most small business owners think of health insurance in terms of networks. A PPO gives employees broad access. An HMO usually limits access to a smaller network. An EPO sits somewhere in between. The insurance carrier negotiates rates with doctors and hospitals, and the employer pays the premium.

Reference-based pricing works differently.

Instead of relying only on a traditional provider network contract, a reference-based pricing plan sets a payment limit based on an outside benchmark. That benchmark is often tied to Medicare.

For example, instead of paying whatever a hospital negotiated with a commercial insurance carrier, the plan might say: “For this service, we will pay 150% of Medicare.”

That sounds simple. It can be powerful. It can also create real problems if employees are not supported properly.

Reference-based pricing, often shortened to RBP, is not usually something a small business buys as a standard fully insured ACA small-group plan. It is more commonly used inside a self-funded or level-funded employer health plan, where the employer has more control over how claims are paid.

For the right employer, RBP can be a serious cost-control strategy. For the wrong employer, it can create employee confusion, provider pushback, and billing disputes.

The value comes from understanding both sides before making the move.

#### What Is Reference-Based Pricing?

Reference-based pricing is a health plan payment method that uses a benchmark to decide how much the plan will pay for certain healthcare services. Nationwide describes reference-based pricing as a plan design that uses Medicare reimbursement rates, or a derived equivalent, as a reference point and prices claims based on a multiple of that rate.

In plain English, the plan does not start with the hospital’s billed charge. It does not necessarily start with a traditional PPO discount either. It starts with a reference point.

That reference point might be Medicare, a percentage above Medicare, another published pricing benchmark, or a direct contract with certain providers.

The most common way to explain it is: Medicare plus a percentage.

For example, a plan might pay a hospital claim at 140%, 150%, or 180% of Medicare. The exact percentage depends on the plan design, vendor, service type, market, and employer strategy.

The goal is not to underpay providers or create conflict. The goal is to avoid paying open-ended commercial prices that may be far above a more transparent benchmark.

#### Why Employers Look at Reference-Based Pricing

Employers look at RBP because commercial healthcare prices can be much higher than Medicare-based prices. RAND’s 2024 Hospital Price Transparency Study found that employers and private insurers paid hospitals an average of about 254% of what Medicare would have paid for the same inpatient and outpatient services in 2022, with wide variation by state and hospital.

That does not mean Medicare always pays the “right” amount. It also does not mean every hospital is doing something wrong.

It does show why employers are frustrated.

If a small business is paying premiums based on commercial hospital prices, and those prices are often two to three times Medicare, the owner may reasonably want a more disciplined way to pay for care. Reference-based pricing is one way to approach that problem.

#### How Reference-Based Pricing Controls Costs

Reference-based pricing controls costs by changing the starting point for payment.

In a traditional PPO arrangement, the employer often sees the negotiated discount but may not know whether the final allowed amount is actually reasonable. A 40% discount sounds good, but if the starting charge is very high, the final price may still be expensive.

RBP tries to avoid that problem by using a clearer benchmark.

Instead of asking whether the plan received a discount off a hospital’s billed charge, RBP asks how the payment compares to a known reference point. That can help control costs in several ways.

It creates a clearer ceiling on certain claims. It makes the pricing logic easier to explain. It may also reduce the plan’s exposure to extremely high facility prices, especially for hospital-based care.

This is why RBP is most often discussed around hospital and facility claims. That is where commercial prices can vary widely and where employer-sponsored plans may pay much more than Medicare.

#### How Doctors and Hospitals Get Paid Under RBP

In a traditional PPO plan, the provider has a contract with the insurance network. The provider agrees to accept the negotiated allowed amount, and the employee pays the plan’s normal cost-sharing. Reference-based pricing may work differently.

If there is no network contract with the provider, the plan pays the claim according to its reference-based pricing formula. The provider may accept the payment. Or the provider may object and try to bill the employee for the difference between the billed charge and what the plan paid.

That difference is often called a balance bill.

This is the biggest practical issue with RBP.

A good RBP program needs more than a pricing formula. It needs member support, provider communication, claim negotiation, legal support where appropriate, and a clear process for handling provider disputes. Without that support, employees may feel caught between the health plan and the provider.

#### The Main Problem: Balance Billing Risk

Reference-based pricing can reduce plan costs, but it can also increase the chance of billing disputes. If a provider does not have a contract requiring it to accept the plan’s payment amount, the provider may bill the employee for the remaining balance.

The federal No Surprises Act protects patients from many surprise bills in emergency situations and certain non-emergency situations involving out-of-network providers at in-network facilities. CMS describes the law as providing protections against surprise billing and a process for resolving out-of-network payment disputes between providers and health plans.

Those protections are important, but they do not eliminate every possible billing dispute.

For example, if an employee voluntarily uses a non-contracted provider for a planned service, and that service is not protected by the No Surprises Act or another applicable law, there may still be billing risk. So RBP should not be presented as “use any provider and everything will be fine.” A more honest explanation is: Reference-based pricing can control costs, but it needs a strong process to protect employees from confusion and billing disputes.

#### Why Employee Education Matters

RBP can fail if employees do not understand how to use the plan.

In a PPO, employees are used to asking whether a provider is in network. In an RBP plan, the process may be different. Employees may need to contact the plan before scheduled procedures, confirm how the claim will be handled, and know what to do if a provider sends a balance bill.

That is a big change. Employees need clear instructions before elective procedures, imaging, surgeries, or hospital-based care. They should know who to call, how to confirm provider acceptance, and what to do if they receive a confusing bill.

A strong RBP strategy should include clear employee materials, a member advocacy team, help before scheduled procedures, support with balance bills, and a process for negotiating disputed claims. The plan design is only part of the solution. The service model matters just as much.

#### Why RBP Usually Comes Through ERISA, Self-Funded, or Level-Funded Plans

Reference-based pricing is not usually offered as a typical fully insured ACA small-group product. A fully insured small-group plan is an insurance policy issued by a carrier. The employer pays a premium, and the insurer takes the claims risk.

RBP is more commonly used in self-funded health plans. KFF explains that self-funded plans are generally regulated under federal law and are largely not required to comply with state insurance laws that apply to fully insured coverage.

ERISA is the federal law that sets minimum standards for most private-sector employer health plans. The Department of Labor explains that ERISA requires plans to provide plan information, includes fiduciary responsibilities, requires a claims and appeals process, and gives participants rights to sue for benefits and fiduciary breaches.

This matters because a self-funded ERISA plan gives the employer more flexibility in plan design than a standard fully insured ACA small-group policy. That does not mean the ACA disappears. Many federal requirements still apply to self-funded plans. But the structure is different from buying a fully insured small-group insurance policy.

For small employers, the most realistic access point is often level funding.

#### How Small Businesses Can Access RBP Through Level-Funded Plans

Level-funded plans are designed to feel more predictable than traditional self-funding while still being built on a self-funded structure.

KFF describes level-funded arrangements as nominally self-funded options that package a self-funded plan with extensive stop-loss coverage. The employer pays a set monthly amount that typically includes expected claims, administrative fees, and stop-loss protection. KFF also reported that in 2025, 37% of covered workers in firms with 10 to 199 workers were enrolled in a level-funded plan.

That is important for small businesses. Historically, more advanced cost-control strategies were easier for large employers to access. Level-funded arrangements have made some self-funded tools more available to smaller employers.

A small employer may access reference-based pricing through a level-funded plan that uses an RBP payment model, a self-funded plan administered by a third-party administrator, a hybrid arrangement that uses a network for some services and RBP for others, or a cost-containment vendor layered into the plan.

Stop-loss insurance is a key part of the structure. KFF explains that stop-loss coverage limits the amount a plan sponsor must pay in claims and may apply to individual high-cost claims or total plan claims. That protection matters because small employers cannot afford unlimited claims exposure.

#### RBP vs. PPO: The Tradeoff

A PPO plan gives employees familiarity. They can search a provider directory, stay in network, and usually avoid most balance-billing concerns for in-network care.

Reference-based pricing may create lower claim costs, especially on hospital and facility claims. But it may require more employee support and more active claim management.

This is not a simple “PPO bad, RBP good” comparison.

A PPO may be the better fit when employees need broad access, when recruiting matters heavily, or when the employer wants a more familiar health plan experience.

RBP may be worth considering when the employer wants a more aggressive cost-control strategy and has the right administrative support behind it.

#### What Could Go Wrong?

RBP has real drawbacks.

Employees may feel anxious if the plan does not work like a familiar PPO. Providers may push back on the payment amount, especially if it is far below their billed charge. Balance bills can create frustration even if the plan has a process for handling them.

The model can also create administrative complexity. A weak RBP vendor or poor member support can make the plan feel chaotic. That is a serious risk for a small business that does not have a large HR department.

Recruiting and retention also matter. A company competing for highly skilled employees may need to be careful before moving away from a familiar PPO-style model.

These problems do not make RBP unusable. They mean it should be handled carefully.

#### When Reference-Based Pricing May Make Sense

Reference-based pricing may be worth reviewing when a small business is facing repeated premium increases, has engaged ownership, and is open to a more hands-on health plan strategy. It may be especially relevant when the employer is already considering level funding and wants more transparency around claims costs.

RBP may be a poor fit when employees strongly value a traditional network, when the employer does not want to deal with billing disputes, or when the company lacks the time and support needed to explain the plan well. A business owner should not choose RBP simply because it looks cheaper on paper.

The better standard is practical fit: meaningful cost control, strong employee support, and a plan structure the employer can actually manage.

#### The Bottom Line

Reference-based pricing is a health plan payment strategy that uses a benchmark, often Medicare, to decide how much the plan will pay for certain healthcare services.

It can control costs by avoiding open-ended commercial pricing and tying payments to a more transparent reference point. That matters because employer-sponsored commercial plans often pay hospitals far more than Medicare-based amounts. RAND’s 2024 study found that employers and private insurers paid hospitals an average of about 254% of Medicare for the same inpatient and outpatient services in 2022.

But RBP is not simple. It can create provider disputes, employee confusion, and balance-billing risk if not managed well. It requires strong administration, clear communication, and member advocacy.

For small businesses, the most realistic way to explore reference-based pricing is usually through a self-funded or level-funded ERISA health plan, not a standard fully insured ACA small-group policy.

For the right employer, RBP can be a serious cost-control tool. For the wrong employer, it can create more frustration than savings.

The goal is not to chase the most aggressive pricing model. The goal is to choose a health plan structure that balances cost control, employee access, risk protection, and practical administration.

#### FAQ SECTION

\1. What is reference-based pricing in health insurance?

Reference-based pricing is a payment method where a health plan sets payment amounts based on an outside benchmark, often Medicare. The plan may pay a percentage above Medicare instead of relying only on traditional PPO network rates.

\2. How does reference-based pricing control costs?

It controls costs by tying payments to a benchmark rather than open-ended billed charges or higher commercial network rates. This can be especially useful for hospital and facility claims.

\3. Is reference-based pricing the same as a PPO?

No. A PPO relies on negotiated provider network contracts. RBP may use a benchmark payment method and may not rely on the same kind of traditional provider network.

\4. Can employees be balance billed under reference-based pricing?

Yes, balance billing can be a risk when there is no provider contract and no legal protection applies. The No Surprises Act protects consumers in many emergency and certain out-of-network situations, but it does not eliminate every possible billing dispute.

\5. Is reference-based pricing available in fully insured ACA small-group plans?

Usually, no. RBP is more commonly used in self-funded or level-funded employer health plans. Fully insured ACA small-group plans are structured differently and are issued as insurance policies.

\6. How can a small business access reference-based pricing?

A small business may access RBP through a level-funded or self-funded plan, often with a third-party administrator, stop-loss coverage, and a cost-containment vendor.

\7. Are level-funded plans considered self-funded?

KFF describes level-funded arrangements as nominally self-funded options that package a self-funded plan with stop-loss coverage. They often involve a set monthly payment that includes expected claims, administrative fees, and stop-loss protection.

\8. Is reference-based pricing a good idea for every small business?

No. RBP can lower costs, but it requires strong employee communication, billing support, and tolerance for a less traditional model. It is not a good fit for every employer.
