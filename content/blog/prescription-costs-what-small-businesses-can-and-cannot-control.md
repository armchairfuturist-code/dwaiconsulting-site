Prescription drug costs are one of the most frustrating parts of health insurance.

Employees feel it when they go to the pharmacy. One person may pay a small copay for a generic drug. Another may face a much higher cost for a brand-name or specialty medication.

Employers feel it differently. A small business owner may see the health insurance renewal increase and wonder how much of the increase is tied to prescriptions. But in many fully insured small-group plans, the owner does not get much detail and does not have much control over the drug program.

That is the important starting point. A small business owner can understand prescription drug costs. They can compare plans. They can ask better renewal questions. They can help employees understand how the plan works. But if the company is in a standard fully insured ACA small-group plan, the employer usually cannot rebuild the prescription drug program from scratch.

That does not make the topic useless. It makes it more important to understand. Prescription drug costs are one of the places where the difference between fully insured and level-funded health plans becomes clearer.

#### Why Prescription Drug Costs Matter

Prescription drugs are part of the claims cost inside a health insurance premium. Some drugs are inexpensive. Others can cost hundreds, thousands, or even tens of thousands of dollars per month. For a small company, one high-cost prescription can matter more than many routine doctor visits.

KFF’s 2025 Employer Health Benefits Survey notes that nearly all covered workers are at firms that provide prescription drug coverage in their largest health plan. KFF also explains that employer plans commonly use drug formularies, cost-sharing tiers, and other tools to manage prescription coverage. That means prescription drug coverage is not one simple benefit.

A health plan may treat drugs differently depending on whether they are generic, brand-name, non-preferred, specialty, or subject to special rules. To an employee, that may feel confusing. To an employer, it matters because the drug program affects both employee experience and plan cost.

#### Generic, Brand, and Specialty Drugs

A simple way to understand prescription drug cost is to divide drugs into three broad categories.

Generic drugs are usually the lowest-cost option. These are medications whose patents have expired and may be made by multiple companies.

Brand-name drugs are usually more expensive. Some have generic alternatives. Others do not.

Specialty drugs are often the biggest concern for health plans. KFF describes specialty drugs as high-cost drugs that may be used for complex or chronic conditions, and they may require special handling or special pharmacy rules.

Specialty drugs are where small employers can get surprised. A company with 20 employees does not need many high-cost prescriptions to feel pressure. One specialty medication may create a large claim all by itself. That does not mean the employee is doing anything wrong. People need the medications they need. But from a plan-cost standpoint, specialty drugs can be a major driver.

#### GLP-1 Drugs Made This More Visible

GLP-1 drugs have made prescription drug costs a bigger topic for employers. These drugs may be used for type 2 diabetes, and some are also used for weight loss. Common examples include Ozempic, Wegovy, Mounjaro, Saxenda, and similar medications.

KFF has noted that employers are paying close attention to GLP-1 coverage because of the cost of the drugs and the possibility that people may use them long term.

This does not mean GLP-1 drugs are bad. For some people, they may be very helpful. The issue for employers is different: Who pays, under what rules, and how does it affect the plan over time?

Large employers may have more data and more ability to adjust coverage strategy. Small employers in fully insured plans usually have less control.

#### Why Fully Insured Small Employers Have Limited Control

In a fully insured ACA small-group plan, the employer is mostly buying a finished product. The insurance carrier controls the prescription drug program. That usually includes the drug list, drug tiers, prior authorization rules, specialty pharmacy rules, and pharmacy benefit manager arrangement.

The employer can choose among available plans, but usually cannot say: “Keep this medical plan, but remove this specialty drug.” Or: “Use this carrier, but let us choose our own prescription drug manager.” That is usually not how fully insured small-group coverage works.

There is also an ACA issue. Non-grandfathered individual and small-group health insurance plans must cover essential health benefits, and prescription drugs are one of the required essential health benefit categories. So in fully insured small-group coverage, the employer can usually choose between available drug programs. The employer usually cannot redesign the drug program.

#### What Is a PBM?

A pharmacy benefit manager, often called a PBM, is a company that helps run the prescription drug part of a health plan.

The PBM may help decide which drugs are covered, which drugs are preferred, what employees pay at the pharmacy, whether prior authorization is required, and which pharmacies employees can use. NAIC describes PBMs as third-party companies that act as intermediaries between insurance carriers and pharmaceutical manufacturers. NAIC also notes that PBMs may create formularies, negotiate rebates, process claims, create pharmacy networks, review drug use, and manage mail-order or specialty pharmacies. In a fully insured small-group plan, the employer usually does not choose the PBM directly. The carrier has already built the prescription drug program into the plan.

That is why small employers often have limited control. They are not managing the drug program themselves. They are choosing from the drug programs attached to the plans available in their market.

#### Why Fully Insured Plans Can Feel Disconnected From Your Own Drug Costs

In a fully insured small-group plan, your premium is not based only on your own company’s prescription drug use. You are buying into a larger insurance pool. That pool includes many employers and many employees. Some may use very few prescriptions. Others may use expensive brand-name drugs, specialty medications, or long-term therapies. That pooling is part of what makes fully insured coverage stable. If one of your employees suddenly needs a very expensive medication, the carrier takes on that risk. Your company is not directly paying that claim out of pocket.

But there is a tradeoff.

If your group has low prescription drug use, you usually do not get a direct reward for that. You are still paying into a premium structure that reflects the carrier’s broader expected drug costs. This is one reason some healthier or lower-claim small businesses compare level-funded plans.

In a level-funded arrangement, the employer’s cost may be more closely connected to its own group’s expected and actual claims experience. That can help some groups, especially if their own risk profile is better than the broader small-group pool.

But this needs to be said clearly: Level funding does not eliminate prescription drug risk.

If someone is already using a high-cost drug, that may affect the quote. If someone starts using one later, that may affect claims or future pricing. Level funding may offer more visibility and more tools. It is not a magic way to avoid all future drug costs.

#### What Small Employers Can Still Do in Fully Insured Plans

Even with limited control, small employers are not helpless.

The first step is to compare available plans. Different carriers and plans may handle prescriptions differently. One plan may have lower copays but a higher premium. Another may have a deductible that applies before drug coverage begins. Another may place specialty drugs on a separate tier.

The second step is to understand how employees are likely to experience the drug benefit. Are common medications easy to fill? Are specialty drugs handled through a specialty pharmacy? Are prior authorizations required? Are there preferred pharmacies or mail-order options?

The third step is employee education. Employees should know how to ask doctors about generic alternatives, preferred drugs, and lower-cost options. They should also understand that using a discount card outside the plan may not count toward their deductible or out-of-pocket maximum.

These steps may not transform the employer’s premium. But they can reduce confusion and help employees use the plan better.

#### Where Level-Funded and Self-Funded Plans May Offer More Control

The bigger change happens when an employer moves away from a standard fully insured plan. In a level-funded or self-funded plan, the employer may have more flexibility, depending on the carrier, third-party administrator, PBM, stop-loss carrier, and plan design.

KFF describes level-funded arrangements as self-funded-style plans packaged with stop-loss coverage, where the employer typically pays a set monthly amount that includes expected claims, administrative fees, and stop-loss protection. KFF also reported that in 2025, 37% of covered workers in firms with 10 to 199 workers were enrolled in a level-funded plan.

This matters because the employer may no longer be buying the same kind of pooled, fully insured drug program. The plan may be priced more closely around the group’s own expected risk and claims experience. That can help some small businesses. It may also create more room to use prescription drug cost-control tools.

Those tools may include stronger specialty drug management, more transparent PBM arrangements, different formulary strategies, prior authorization rules, step therapy, site-of-care management for infused drugs, or specialty pharmacy programs.

Some plans may also explore specialty drug carve-outs or alternative funding strategies. But more control also means more responsibility. A plan that aggressively manages prescription drugs may lower costs, but it can also create employee confusion, access concerns, or administrative problems if it is not handled well.

#### Specialty Drug Carve-Outs Need Careful Review

A specialty drug carve-out means certain high-cost drugs are handled differently from the standard prescription drug program. That may sound attractive. If a category of drugs is driving cost, why not manage it separately? Sometimes that may be worth reviewing. But it is not a simple fix.

A carve-out can create legal, administrative, and employee experience issues. The plan document must be clear. Claims and appeals rules matter. Employees need to understand what is covered and what process they need to follow.

ERISA also matters. The Department of Labor explains that ERISA sets standards for people who manage employee benefit plans and that an ERISA-covered group health plan can include prescription drug coverage. So the message should not be: “Carve out specialty drugs and save money.” A better message is: “Some level-funded or self-funded plans may offer more prescription drug cost-control tools, but those tools need careful review.”

#### PBMs, Rebates, and Why This Gets Complicated

Prescription drug pricing can be hard to see clearly. The price paid at the pharmacy, the rebate from the drug manufacturer, the employee’s copay, and the plan’s net cost may all be different numbers. PBMs are part of that system. They may negotiate rebates, build formularies, process claims, and manage pharmacy networks.

In a fully insured small-group plan, the employer usually has limited visibility into this system. In a self-funded or level-funded plan, the employer may get more information. But that depends on the plan, PBM, carrier, administrator, and reporting. This is why prescription drug strategy is not just about telling employees to choose cheaper drugs. It is also about plan structure, transparency, and administration.

#### A Simple Example

Imagine a 25-person professional firm in Illinois.

The company has a fully insured small-group health plan. One employee uses a specialty medication. Another employee is interested in a GLP-1 medication. The owner sees the renewal increase and wonders whether prescriptions are part of the problem. In the fully insured plan, the owner probably cannot remove a drug category, change the PBM, or redesign the specialty drug program. The owner can compare available plans and review how each handles prescriptions.

Now imagine the firm also reviews a level-funded option.

That option may provide more visibility and more prescription drug management tools. It may have stronger rules around specialty drugs, prior authorization, preferred pharmacies, or site-of-care management. It may also come with more complexity.

Neither option is automatically better. The fully insured plan may be simpler and more familiar. The level-funded plan may offer more control and may better reflect the group’s own risk. The best fit depends on the employer’s workforce, risk tolerance, claims profile, and need for predictability.

#### The Bottom Line

Prescription drug costs are an important part of health insurance premiums. Some prescriptions are inexpensive. Others, especially specialty drugs and certain newer therapies, can create major cost pressure. For fully insured small-group plans, small employers usually have limited control. They can compare carriers and plan designs, but they generally cannot rebuild the drug program. They are also paying into a broader insurance pool. That gives them protection if one of their own employees needs an expensive medication, but it also means they may be paying for broader expected drug costs even when their own group has low drug use.

Level-funded and self-funded plans may offer more tools. These can include specialty drug management, PBM transparency, prior authorization, site-of-care rules, and sometimes specialty carve-out strategies. But those tools require careful review, good administration, and clear employee communication.

For small businesses, the right approach is not to chase every aggressive cost-control idea. The right approach is to understand what the current plan allows, what alternatives are available, and whether more control is worth the added complexity.

#### FAQ SECTION

\1. Do prescription drugs affect small business health insurance premiums?

Yes. Prescription drugs are part of the claims cost inside a health plan. High-cost specialty drugs can create meaningful pressure on plan costs, especially for small groups.

\2. Can a small employer control prescription costs in a fully insured plan?

Only in limited ways. A small employer can compare available carriers and plan designs, but the carrier usually controls the formulary, PBM, drug tiers, prior authorization rules, and specialty pharmacy rules.

\3. Are prescription drugs required in ACA small-group plans?

Yes. Non-grandfathered individual and small-group health insurance plans must cover essential health benefits, and prescription drugs are one of the required benefit categories.

\4. What is a PBM?

A PBM, or pharmacy benefit manager, helps manage the prescription drug part of a health plan. PBMs may create formularies, process claims, negotiate rebates, create pharmacy networks, and manage specialty or mail-order pharmacy programs.

\5. What are specialty drugs?

Specialty drugs are high-cost medications often used for complex or chronic conditions. They may require special handling, special approval, or use of a specialty pharmacy.

\6. Can level-funded plans offer more prescription drug control?

Sometimes. Level-funded or self-funded plans may allow more flexibility around PBM arrangements, specialty drugs, prior authorization, and other cost-control tools. The details depend on the plan, vendor, contract, and stop-loss arrangement.

\7. What is a specialty drug carve-out?

A specialty drug carve-out means certain high-cost drugs are handled outside the standard drug program or managed under a separate strategy. This can create savings opportunities, but it can also create legal, administrative, and employee communication issues.

\8. Should every small business try to carve out specialty drugs?

No. Specialty drug carve-outs need careful review. They may not fit every employer and should be evaluated with qualified plan, legal, and administrative guidance.
