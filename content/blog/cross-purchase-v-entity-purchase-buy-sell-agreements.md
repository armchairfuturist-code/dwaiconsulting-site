For businesses with multiple owners, one of the most important decisions in buy-sell planning is determining how ownership interests will be transferred if an owner dies, becomes disabled, retires, or unexpectedly exits the business.

Two of the most common structures are cross-purchase agreements and entity-purchase agreements. While both are designed to help facilitate ownership transition and business continuity, they operate differently and may create different administrative, financial, and tax considerations.

For many closely held businesses, understanding the differences between these structures is an important part of long-term planning.

#### What Is a Cross-Purchase Agreement?

In a cross-purchase agreement, the surviving owners agree to purchase the departing owner’s ownership interest directly.

For example, if a business has three equal owners and one owner dies, the remaining two owners would personally purchase the deceased owner’s share according to the terms of the agreement.

Life insurance is commonly used to fund these arrangements. In many cases, each owner owns policies on the other owners so funds are available to complete the purchase if an owner dies unexpectedly.

Cross-purchase arrangements are often viewed as attractive for businesses with a smaller number of owners because they can provide certain tax basis advantages for the surviving owners. After the purchase, the remaining owners generally receive an increase in basis tied to the acquired ownership interest. However, administration can become more complicated as the number of owners increases. Multiple policies may be required, and managing ownership, premium payments, and policy coordination can become increasingly complex over time.

#### What Is an Entity-Purchase Agreement?

In an entity-purchase agreement, sometimes called a stock redemption agreement, the business itself agrees to purchase the departing owner’s interest.

Rather than the surviving owners personally buying the ownership share, the company redeems the ownership interest directly.

Under this structure, the business commonly owns the life insurance policies on the owners and receives the proceeds if an owner dies. The business then uses those funds to purchase the departing owner’s interest according to the agreement terms.

Entity-purchase arrangements are often viewed as simpler from an administrative perspective, particularly for businesses with multiple owners. Instead of requiring owners to maintain policies on one another, the business manages the policies centrally. However, depending on the business structure and tax situation, entity-purchase agreements may not provide the same basis adjustment advantages that can exist under certain cross-purchase arrangements.

#### Why Businesses Evaluate Both Structures

The appropriate structure depends on many factors, including: number of owners, business entity type, ownership percentages, long-term succession goals, administrative preferences, funding strategy and tax considerations

For example, a two-owner engineering firm may evaluate planning differently than a manufacturing company with five owners or a growing contractor business with future ownership transition plans.

In many cases, businesses also evaluate: whether ownership is expected to remain stable, whether future owners may be added, how valuation will be determined, how disability or retirement situations will be handled, and whether the business itself has sufficient liquidity

Because buy-sell planning affects both ownership continuity and long-term business operations, these decisions are often more significant than simply selecting an insurance product.

#### Why Life Insurance Is Commonly Used

Life insurance is frequently used in both cross-purchase and entity-purchase agreements because it can provide immediate liquidity during an otherwise financially stressful situation.

Without funding in place, surviving owners or the business itself may struggle to:

- Purchase ownership interests
- Maintain cash flow
- Satisfy estate obligations
- Preserve operational stability

Insurance funding helps create a mechanism for ownership transfer while reducing financial strain during a difficult transition period.

#### Planning Considerations Matter

Many businesses postpone buy-sell planning because ownership transitions feel distant or uncomfortable to discuss. However, uncertainty surrounding ownership transfer can create significant operational and financial problems if planning has not been established before a triggering event occurs.

Properly structured agreements help establish the transition procedures, valuation methods, funding and long-term success.

For many closely held businesses, buy-sell planning is less about insurance products and more about reducing uncertainty during one of the most difficult situations a business can face.

#### Final Thoughts

Both cross-purchase and entity-purchase agreements can provide effective frameworks for ownership continuity when properly structured. The right approach depends on the size of the business, ownership structure, long-term planning goals, and overall succession strategy.

For businesses with multiple owners, evaluating these structures before a major life event occurs can help create greater clarity, stability, and long-term continuity for owners, employees, clients, and family members alike.
