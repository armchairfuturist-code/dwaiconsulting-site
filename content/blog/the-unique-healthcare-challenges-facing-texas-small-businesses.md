Texas businesses operate in one of the most difficult healthcare environments in the country. Rising insurance costs, limited network options, high uninsured rates, and affordability concerns continue placing pressure on both employers and employees.

For small businesses trying to recruit and retain workers while managing long-term costs, these challenges increasingly affect hiring, employee satisfaction, and financial planning decisions.

#### Texas Continues to Have One of the Highest Uninsured Rates in the Country

Texas consistently reports the highest uninsured rate in the United States. According to data from the Kaiser Family Foundation, approximately 19% of Texans remain uninsured, significantly higher than the national average. (kff.org)

This impacts not only individuals, but also employers competing for workers in industries where access to health coverage can influence recruiting and retention decisions.

Businesses without strong healthcare options may find it more difficult to attract experienced employees, particularly in competitive industries such as engineering, construction, manufacturing, and technology.

#### Healthcare Affordability Remains a Major Concern

Healthcare affordability continues to be a growing issue across Texas. Premiums, deductibles, and out-of-pocket costs have increased steadily over time, placing pressure on both employers and employees.

Recent studies have shown many Texas families spend more than 10% of household income on healthcare-related expenses, including premiums and deductibles. (axios.com)

For small businesses, annual renewal increases often create difficult tradeoffs between maintaining affordable employee contributions, preserving coverage quality, controlling long-term operating costs, and remaining competitive when hiring new employees. Many employers express frustration that rising costs frequently appear disconnected from the actual demographics or utilization patterns of their workforce.

#### Many Texans Delay or Avoid Medical Care Due to Cost

Cost concerns also affect healthcare utilization. Surveys continue to show that many Texans delay treatment, skip preventive care, or avoid filling prescriptions because of affordability concerns.

When employees postpone care, businesses can experience indirect impacts through increased absenteeism, lower productivity, delayed diagnoses, and higher long-term claims severity. Over time, healthcare affordability challenges can affect both employee wellbeing and overall employer healthcare costs.

#### Mental Health Access Remains a Significant Issue

Texas also faces ongoing shortages in mental health access. Many counties continue experiencing shortages of mental health professionals, and employees frequently encounter long wait times or limited provider availability.

These challenges increasingly affect workplace productivity, retention, burnout, morale, and disability-related concerns. As a result, provider network access and overall plan design have become increasingly important considerations for employers evaluating health insurance options.

#### Marketplace Network Limitations Create Additional Frustration

In many parts of Texas, individual marketplace plans are heavily concentrated in HMO and EPO structures, which may limit out-of-network access and reduce provider flexibility compared to broader PPO arrangements.

Employees who are accustomed to PPO flexibility often find marketplace alternatives restrictive, particularly when they travel frequently, rely on specialist care, or want broader physician access. For many employers, maintaining strong provider networks continues to be an important part of recruiting and retaining skilled employees.

#### Small Businesses Face Difficult Long-Term Decisions

For many Texas employers, healthcare challenges have evolved beyond simple annual premium shopping.

Businesses are increasingly evaluating long-term cost sustainability, PPO network access, workforce demographics, employee retention concerns, funding structure alternatives, and the predictability of future renewals.

This has contributed to growing interest in alternative funding structures such as level-funded health plans, particularly among businesses with approximately 10–50 employees.

While level-funded plans are not appropriate for every business, some employers are exploring whether alternative funding models may better align with their workforce profile and long-term planning goals.

#### Why These Challenges Matter to Employers

Healthcare costs now influence far more than insurance budgets alone. For many small businesses, employee benefits directly affect recruiting, retention, workplace stability, employee satisfaction, long-term operating costs, and overall business competitiveness.

As healthcare costs and access concerns continue evolving across Texas, employers are increasingly looking for strategies that balance affordability, provider access, and long-term sustainability in a difficult and rapidly changing market.

#### How Level-Funded Health Plans Are Becoming Part of the Conversation

As healthcare affordability pressures continue growing across Texas, many small businesses are beginning to evaluate whether alternative funding structures may provide more flexibility than traditional fully insured plans or limited-network marketplace options.

One of the primary reasons employers explore level-funded health plans is the potential for broader PPO network access while maintaining more predictable monthly budgeting.

In many areas of Texas, individual market coverage is heavily concentrated in HMO and EPO structures that can limit physician access and out-of-network flexibility. Employees who are accustomed to PPO plans often view network access as an important part of their overall compensation and benefits package.

For employers trying to recruit and retain skilled workers, particularly in industries such as engineering, construction, manufacturing, and professional services, maintaining strong provider access can become an important competitive factor.

Level-funded plans frequently provide access to larger PPO networks while operating under a different funding structure than traditional fully insured small-group plans.

These arrangements combine predictable monthly payments, stop-loss protection, underwriting considerations, and alternative funding mechanics into a structure designed to provide more stable budgeting while evaluating risk differently than traditional community-rated plans.

Rather than relying entirely on broader community-rated pricing structures, level-funded plans may allow pricing to align more closely with certain workforce demographic characteristics and utilization expectations.

For some businesses with favorable workforce profiles, this can create opportunities to evaluate long-term affordability, renewal sustainability, PPO network access, additional cost transparency, and alternative approaches to managing healthcare expenses.

Level-funded plans are not appropriate for every business and do not automatically reduce costs in every situation. Factors such as employee age, participation levels, industry type, geographic location, and historical utilization patterns all influence underwriting and long-term performance.

However, as Texas employers continue facing rising premiums, affordability concerns, and provider network frustrations, level-funded health plans are becoming an increasingly common part of long-term employee benefits discussions for businesses with approximately 10–50 employees.
