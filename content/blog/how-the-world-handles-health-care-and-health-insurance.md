Americans often talk about health care as if there are only two choices: the U.S. private insurance system or “government-run health care.”

That is too simple.

Most wealthy countries use some mix of public funding, private doctors, private hospitals, private insurance, employer contributions, taxes, patient cost-sharing, and government rules. The difference is not whether private health care exists. It does almost everywhere. The real difference is how each country builds the floor underneath people when they get sick.

The United States has some of the strongest private insurance protections in the world for people who are already inside ACA-compliant major medical coverage. For example, Marketplace plans cannot reject someone, charge more, or refuse to cover essential health benefits because of a pre-existing condition. Medicaid and CHIP also cannot deny coverage or charge more because of a pre-existing condition.

That is a major protection.

But the U.S. also has a fragmented system where coverage depends heavily on job status, income, age, state rules, employer decisions, enrollment windows, plan networks, deductibles, and program eligibility. So the American system can be very generous once someone is in the right plan, but it does not create the same universal floor that many other countries build into their national systems.

That is the key difference.

## The U.S. System: Strong Insurance Rights, Weak Universal Floor

The U.S. system is not purely private. It is a patchwork.

Some people are covered by employer-sponsored insurance. Some are covered by Medicare, Medicaid, CHIP, ACA Marketplace plans, VA coverage, TRICARE, or other public programs. Some people have no coverage at all. Others technically have coverage but still face high deductibles, narrow networks, prior authorization, pharmacy restrictions, or out-of-network costs.

This creates a strange contradiction. The U.S. has strong rules against pre-existing-condition exclusions in ACA-compliant coverage, but it still leaves people exposed to high costs and coverage gaps.

The U.S. also spends far more than other wealthy countries. OECD data for 2025 showed U.S. health spending at about $14,885 per person, compared with an OECD average of about $5,967. Health spending was about 17.2% of U.S. GDP, compared with 9.3% across the OECD.

So the issue is not that the U.S. refuses to spend money on health care. The issue is that the money moves through a complicated mix of taxes, employer premiums, employee premiums, deductibles, copays, coinsurance, public programs, subsidies, and private contracts.

In plain English: Americans pay taxes for health care, then often pay premiums, then often pay again when they use care.

## Portugal: A Public Safety Net With Private Options on Top

Portugal is a good contrast.

Portugal’s public health system, the Serviço Nacional de Saúde, or SNS, is primarily tax-funded and provides universal coverage. The European Observatory describes Portugal as having a predominantly tax-based health system with universal coverage, with general practitioners acting as gatekeepers to specialist and hospital care.

That means the basic question in Portugal is not usually, “Will an insurer take me with this medical condition?”

The question is more often, “Am I properly registered in the public system, and how quickly can I access the care I need?”

That is a very different structure from the U.S.

Portugal has also removed most user fees in the SNS. Since June 2022, user fees ended for almost all SNS services, remaining mainly for emergency care without prior SNS referral unless the patient is admitted afterward.

But that does not mean Portugal has unlimited care with no tradeoffs. Portugal still has access issues, wait times, referral pathways, and limits on what is fully covered. Private insurance and private care are often used for speed, convenience, specialist access, dental, vision, diagnostics, and services that people do not want to wait for in the public system.

This is one of the biggest lessons from looking internationally: universal coverage does not mean unlimited coverage.

It means there is a baseline system that does not depend on your employer, your health history, or whether you happen to be inside the right insurance contract.

## Germany: Payroll-Funded Social Insurance

Germany uses a different model. It is not a British-style national health service and not an American-style employer insurance market.

Germany has mandatory health insurance with near-universal coverage. Most residents are covered through statutory health insurance funds, often called sickness funds, which are financed through compulsory wage contributions shared by employers and employees.

This model feels closer to payroll-based social insurance. Workers and employers pay into the system, and the system spreads risk across the population.

The important point is that Germany still uses insurers. It still has private-sector elements. It still has employer involvement. But it does not leave basic health coverage as a voluntary employer benefit in the same way the U.S. does.

Health care is treated more like part of the country’s social insurance structure.

## The Netherlands and Switzerland: Private Insurance, But Not American-Style

The Netherlands and Switzerland are often mentioned by Americans because they use private insurance. But their private insurance systems are much more tightly regulated than the U.S. system.

In the Netherlands, everyone who lives or works there must take out standard health insurance. The government decides what the standard package covers, insurers must accept anyone who applies for the standard package, and they must charge the same premium regardless of age or health status. Additional insurance is optional, and insurers have more freedom to ask health questions or refuse applicants for those extra benefits.

That distinction matters.

The Dutch system uses private insurance companies, but the basic package functions like a heavily regulated public obligation. Insurers compete inside a government-defined framework.

That is very different from the American idea of private insurance as a contract that can vary dramatically by employer, state, network, deductible, carrier, and plan design.

## Other Countries Have Limits. They Just Use Different Limits.

One mistake Americans sometimes make is assuming other countries cover everything.

They do not.

Every health system limits care in some way because no country can pay for every service, every drug, every provider, every hospital, and every treatment immediately for everyone.

The difference is how the limits show up.

In the U.S., limits often appear through insurance design: deductibles, copays, coinsurance, prior authorization, narrow networks, drug formularies, medical necessity reviews, out-of-network penalties, and plan exclusions.

In other countries, limits often appear through public budgets, referral systems, national benefit packages, waiting lists, cost-effectiveness reviews, centralized drug approval, or decisions about which services belong in the public system.

That is why a good U.S. employer plan can sometimes feel better than a public system overseas. It may offer faster access, broader specialist choice, stronger pharmacy coverage, or better dental and vision benefits.

But the tradeoff is that the U.S. does not guarantee that everyone has access to that good plan.

Other countries often provide a stronger floor, but not always the same ceiling.

## The Safety Net Question: Does Covering Everyone Actually Help?

The strongest argument for universal coverage is not that it magically makes health care cheap.

It does not.

The stronger argument is that a universal safety net makes society less fragile.

When people avoid care because of cost, manageable problems can become expensive problems. KFF found that uninsured adults are much more likely than insured adults to delay or skip needed care or medication because of cost.

Medical debt is another major issue. KFF estimates that people in the United States owe at least $220 billion in medical debt, with about 14 million people owing more than $1,000 and about 3 million owing more than $10,000.

That debt does not stay neatly inside the health care system. It affects credit, savings, rent, family stress, job choices, and future care decisions.

A stronger safety net can reduce that kind of damage. It can help people treat diabetes, blood pressure, asthma, cancer, mental health conditions, pregnancy complications, and chronic illness earlier. It can also make workers less dependent on staying in a job solely because they need the health insurance.

That matters for small businesses, too.

In the U.S., small employers are often forced to compete with large employers on benefits. That is hard. Large companies usually have more HR staff, more negotiating power, more predictable risk pools, and more resources to absorb premium increases.

A universal safety net would not eliminate the need for employer benefits, but it would change what employer benefits are expected to do. Employers might still offer supplemental benefits, private access, dental, vision, disability, life insurance, executive benefits, and other perks. But they would not be carrying as much of the burden for basic medical security.

## Does Universal Coverage Save Money?

Sometimes. Not automatically.

Universal coverage can create savings when it helps people get care earlier, reduces avoidable hospitalizations, lowers medical debt, simplifies administration, and gives the system enough bargaining power to control prices.

But if a country covers everyone while still paying very high prices for hospitals, drugs, specialists, administration, and billing complexity, total spending may not fall. More people using care can increase spending unless the system also controls prices and manages access.

That is why other countries use limits. They may rely on referral systems, public budgets, national fee schedules, drug reimbursement rules, or defined benefit packages.

The U.S. also uses limits. We just tend to hide them inside insurance contracts.

So the honest answer is this: universal coverage is not automatically cheaper, but it can be more stable, more predictable, and less financially destructive for families.

## What the U.S. Can Learn From the Rest of the World

The U.S. does not need to pretend every other country has solved health care perfectly. They have not.

Portugal has wait times and public-system capacity issues. Canada has access delays and narrower public coverage for some services. England has budget pressure. Germany has payroll costs. The Netherlands and Switzerland still require people to pay premiums. No system escapes tradeoffs.

But other wealthy countries generally start with a different assumption.

They treat basic health coverage as a public or social obligation first, then allow private insurance or private care to sit on top of that foundation.

The U.S. often does the opposite. It starts with private coverage, employer benefits, program eligibility, and insurance contracts, then tries to patch the gaps with public programs, subsidies, hospital charity care, emergency care rules, and financial assistance.

That is why the U.S. system feels so complicated.

It is not simply private. It is not simply public. It is a very expensive hybrid that relies heavily on employers and insurance contracts to do work that other countries handle through national systems, social insurance, or tightly regulated mandatory coverage.

## The Bottom Line

America is not the only country with private health care, private insurance, hospitals, specialists, networks, premiums, or patient cost-sharing.

What makes the U.S. unusual is the combination of:

employer-based coverage, high prices, fragmented public programs, private insurance complexity, no fully universal coverage guarantee, and significant out-of-pocket exposure.

Other countries have limits, too. They just deal with those limits differently.

The best way to describe the tradeoff is this:

The U.S. can provide excellent coverage for people inside the right plan, but it does not provide the same reliable floor for everyone.

Countries like Portugal provide a stronger public floor, but people may use private insurance or private care for speed, convenience, or services outside the public system.

For business owners, this matters because health care in the U.S. is not just a medical issue. It is a recruiting issue, a payroll issue, a compliance issue, a budgeting issue, and a benefits strategy issue.

That is why small employers need to understand more than just premiums. They need to understand what kind of system they are operating inside — and how to design benefits that protect employees without wasting money.
