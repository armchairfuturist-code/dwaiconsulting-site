A small business owner receives a health insurance renewal and sees one number: The premium.

Maybe it rose 8%. Maybe it rose 18%. Maybe it rose enough to force a serious conversation about changing plans, shifting more cost to employees, or exploring other options. But that one number hides a lot.

A health insurance premium is not just a payment for doctor visits and hospital bills. It is a bundled price that reflects expected medical claims, prescription drug costs, provider network arrangements, the risk of unexpectedly large claims, administrative expenses, taxes and fees, broker compensation, insurer margin, and the need for financial reserves.

In federal rate-review filings, health insurers generally break premiums into broad categories such as projected incurred claims, administrative expenses, taxes and fees, and profit and risk load. Those buckets do not show every detail a business owner might care about, but they are a useful starting point for understanding what is really inside the monthly premium. For small businesses, this matters because health insurance cost is often treated as one unavoidable lump sum. In reality, it is made up of several different pressures. Some are hard for an employer to influence. Others can be affected, at least indirectly, through plan structure, network choice, funding arrangement, and benefits strategy.

Taking a few minutes to read this will provide you with more knowledge than most brokers and help you to take control of your health plan costs.

#### Premiums Are Built Around Expected Claims

The largest part of a health insurance premium is typically the insurer’s estimate of future healthcare claims.

This means the carrier is projecting how much it expects to pay for covered medical services during the coming plan year. In formal rate-development materials, these projected claims are the foundation on which the rest of the premium is built. Administrative expenses, taxes, fees, and profit or risk charges are added around that expected claims base.

For a small business owner, the simplest way to think about it is: The premium starts with the cost of healthcare the carrier expects to pay, then adds the cost of running and protecting the insurance arrangement and can include several categories.

\1. Medical Claims: Hospitals, Doctors, Procedures, and Ongoing Care

The largest visible component inside most health insurance premiums is medical claims. These are the costs tied to covered healthcare services such as:

- Hospital stays
- Surgeries
- Emergency room care
- Outpatient procedures
- Office visits
- Specialist care
- Imaging and lab work
- Behavioral health services
- Ongoing treatment for chronic conditions

Actuarial filings commonly analyze projected claims by categories such as inpatient, outpatient, physician, and prescription drug costs. They also consider utilization (how often members are expected to use those services) and, unit cost (how much those services are expected to cost when used).

This distinction matters. Premiums do not rise only because “people are going to the doctor more.” They can also rise because:

- A hospital system charges more for the same procedure
- More care shifts to expensive outpatient facilities
- Higher-cost imaging or specialty services become more common
- Chronic conditions require more ongoing treatment

When small employers think about rising premiums, this is usually the biggest place to start: what healthcare is expected to cost in the market overall.

\2. Prescription Drug Costs

Prescription drugs are part of claims, but they deserve their own discussion because they have become one of the most visible and misunderstood pieces of health plan cost. Prescription spending can include:

- Common generic medications
- Brand-name drugs
- Specialty medications
- Injectables and biologics
- High-cost therapies for complex conditions

In insurer rate-development work, prescription drug claims are often modeled separately from other medical claim categories because drug trend can behave differently from hospital or physician cost trend. For business owners, the practical point is this: A plan can have relatively ordinary doctor and hospital usage, but a small number of high-cost prescriptions can still create meaningful pressure on claim costs.

This is one reason two groups that look similar from a headcount perspective can have very different expected costs under an underwritten arrangement. It is also why prescription drug management, formulary design, and specialty medication exposure often matter in the broader conversation about health plan cost.

\3. Provider Network Pricing and Utilization

A health insurance premium is also affected by which providers are in the network and how the plan is designed to direct care.

CMS rate-review instructions explicitly identify the plan’s provider network, delivery system characteristics, and utilization management practices as factors that may affect plan-level pricing.

That may sound abstract, but it has a very practical meaning: The same care can cost very different amounts depending on where it is delivered.

A procedure done at one hospital system may cost much more than the same procedure performed through a different contracted facility. A broad network that includes nearly every major hospital may be attractive to employees, but it can also carry higher costs. A more managed or narrower network may lower expected claims, but it can create access concerns if employees want specific doctors or health systems.

Provider network strategy can influence premiums through:

- Negotiated reimbursement rates
- Hospital and physician contracting
- Access to lower-cost settings of care
- Whether the plan steers members toward urgent care, primary care, telehealth, or higher-cost emergency settings
- Prior authorization and other utilization-management practices

\4. Large-Claim Risk and the Cost of Uncertainty

Health insurance premiums do not reflect only the “average” expected year. They also reflect the possibility of a bad year. A serious cancer diagnosis, a premature birth, a transplant, a major accident, or a complex specialty drug regimen can create claims that dwarf ordinary office visits and routine prescriptions. Fully insured carriers take on that risk when they issue the policy.

Rate-review materials recognize this through concepts such as risk and profit load, risk adjustment, high-cost risk pool adjustments, and other mechanisms used to account for uncertainty and unusually large claims in the broader market. For the employer, this shows up as part of the premium rather than as a separate visible charge and that is an important difference between fully insured and level-funded health plans. In a fully insured plan, the employer sees one bundled premium. The carrier owns the claims risk. In a level-funded plan, the pieces are often more visible: expected claims funding, administrative costs, and stop-loss coverage against large claims.

Either way, protection from unpredictable high-cost claims has a price. The difference is how clearly the employer sees it and whether the employer may later participate in better-than-expected claims experience.

\5. Administrative and Operating Costs

Health insurance does not run itself. The insurer also has to pay for the systems and people needed to administer coverage, including:

- Claims processing
- Eligibility and enrollment
- Billing
- Customer service
- Member portals and technology
- Compliance and regulatory reporting
- Provider contracting
- Utilization review
- Care management
- Appeals and grievances
- Data systems and fraud controls

CMS rate-review materials group these costs under administrative expense, and they are part of the non-claims portion of the premium. Some of these expenses are easy for consumers to criticize. Others are essential. A health insurer that cannot process claims accurately, maintain provider contracts, meet regulatory standards, or support members properly would create a different kind of cost problem.

For small employers, the useful question is not simply: “Why is there administration at all?” It is: “What administrative infrastructure are we paying for, and does the plan structure deliver enough value for the cost?”

\6. Broker Compensation, Marketing, and Distribution Costs

Another piece of the premium structure is the cost of distribution.

That can include:

- Broker commissions or compensation
- Sales support
- Proposal systems
- Marketing
- Enrollment support
- Account management

CMS rate instructions refer to distribution and administrative costs as part of the allowable plan-level cost structure. For small business owners, it is worth understanding that advisor compensation is often embedded somewhere in the economics of the health plan, even if it does not appear as a separate invoice.

That does not mean the broker is “adding” an unnecessary layer. A good benefits advisor should help the employer interpret renewals, compare plan structures, understand cost drivers, handle employee questions, avoid poor-fit decisions and coordinate a more thoughtful long-term benefits strategy. But it does mean small businesses should expect real value from the advice they receive.

\7. Taxes, Fees, and Assessments

Health insurance premiums can also include taxes, regulatory fees, and other assessments. CMS rate-review instructions break out taxes and fees as a separate component from administrative expenses and profit/risk load. These may vary depending on the market, jurisdiction, and specific regulatory requirements. For the employer, they are not usually controllable in the same way that plan design or network selection may be. But they are still part of why the premium is higher than claims alone.

This is also why a premium should not be interpreted as just “claims plus profit.” There are multiple layers between the cost of care and the final amount billed.

\8. Profit, Risk Load, or Contribution to Surplus

Health insurers also price for profit, risk margin, contribution to surplus and the possibility that actual claims or expenses exceed projections CMS rate-review materials label this category profit and risk load.

For a for-profit carrier, some amount of margin is expected. For a nonprofit carrier, the comparable concept may be framed more as a contribution to surplus or financial stability rather than shareholder profit. Either way, the insurer is not merely collecting premium and passing through exact medical costs. It is taking insurance risk and maintaining a viable business.

This is also where the ACA’s Medical Loss Ratio, often called the 80/20 rule in the individual and small-group markets, enters the conversation. In general, carriers in those markets must spend at least 80% of premium revenue on medical care and quality-improvement activities, or issue rebates if they fall short. The remaining share is not pure profit; it covers administration, marketing, distribution, and margin.

\9. Reserves, Surplus, and the Ability to Pay Future Claims

Insurance is a promise to pay claims in the future. To make that promise credible, insurers need adequate reserves and surplus.

Reserves are liabilities held for obligations the insurer expects to pay, such as claims that have already occurred but have not yet been fully reported or settled. Surplus provides an additional financial cushion to absorb adverse experience and protect policyholders.

This is not always a clean “line item” inside the premium in the way claims or administrative expenses are. But rates must be sufficient to support the insurer’s broader financial stability over time.

For a small employer, the practical takeaway is simple: Part of what you are buying is not just this month’s healthcare usage. You are buying the carrier’s ability to stand behind the promise when expensive claims hit. That protection has economic value, even though it is easy to overlook during renewal season.

#### Which Premium Components Can a Small Employer Influence?

This is where the topic becomes useful rather than merely educational.

A small employer usually cannot control:

- State and federal fees
- The carrier’s basic need for solvency
- Broad market medical inflation
- Whether someone has a catastrophic diagnosis next year

But an employer may be able to influence or at least evaluate:

- Provider network selection
- Plan design
- Funding structure
- Prescription drug management differences between plan options
- Whether a level-funded arrangement is worth comparing
- Whether current benefits spending aligns with hiring and retention goals

That is why the components matter. They help owners determine What is truly outside their control and what deserves review.

#### Why This Matters for Small Businesses

For a 12-person engineering firm, a 20-person technology company, or a 35-person trade contractor, health insurance is rarely just an HR line item. It is often one of the largest recurring employee-benefit expenses the company faces.

When premiums increase, the owner may feel like the only choices are: Pay more  |  Cut benefits  |  Shift cost to employees

Sometimes those are the only immediate options but, not always. A clearer understanding of what sits inside the premium can lead to better questions:

- Are we paying for a provider network that is broader than employees need?
- Are prescription claims or large-claim risk putting pressure on the plan?
- Would a different funding structure better reward favorable claims experience?
- Are we treating a strategic business expense like a commodity renewal?

Those are much better questions than simply asking for “the cheapest plan.”

#### The Bottom Line

A small business health insurance premium is made up of more than just medical bills. It generally reflects expected medical claims, prescription drug costs, provider network and utilization patterns, the risk of large unpredictable claims, administrative operations, distribution and broker compensation, taxes and regulatory fees, profit or contribution to surplus and financial stability needs such as reserves and solvency support

Federal rate-review materials organize premiums around claims, administrative expense, taxes and fees, and profit/risk load, but for a business owner, the practical story is broader. Your premium is the price of healthcare, insurance risk, and the system required to deliver both.

Understanding that structure will not make health insurance inexpensive. But it can make the next renewal conversation far more productive and it can help identify which cost pressures are worth exploring more deeply.

#### FAQ SECTION

\1. What are the main components of a health insurance premium?

At a high level, health insurance premiums reflect projected claims, administrative expenses, taxes and fees, and profit or risk load. In practical terms, that includes medical claims, prescriptions, provider network costs, operations, distribution costs, and insurer risk protection.

\2. Are medical claims the biggest part of the premium?

Medical and prescription drug claims are typically the core driver of premium development because the insurer begins with projected healthcare costs before adding administrative, tax, and risk-related amounts.

\3. Are prescription drugs included in health insurance premiums?

Yes. Prescription drug costs are part of projected claims, and insurers often analyze them separately from inpatient, outpatient, and physician claims when developing rates.

\4. How do provider networks affect health insurance premiums?

Provider networks can affect premiums through negotiated prices, delivery system design, and utilization-management practices. CMS rate-review instructions identify provider network and delivery system characteristics as factors that can influence plan-level pricing.

\5. Is profit the same as the non-claims portion of premium?

No. In the individual and small-group markets, the ACA Medical Loss Ratio rule generally requires at least 80% of premium revenue to go toward medical care and quality improvement, but the remaining share covers administration, marketing, distribution, and margin — not just profit.

\6. Why is there a cost for large claim risk?

Fully insured carriers assume the risk that actual claims may be worse than expected, including very large and unpredictable claims. Premiums account for that uncertainty through broader risk-pricing mechanisms and risk-related loads.

\7. Are broker commissions included in health insurance premiums?

Distribution costs, which can include broker compensation and related sales expenses, are part of the broader administrative and distribution cost structure used in health insurance pricing.

\8. What parts of the premium can a small business actually influence?

A small employer cannot control every cost driver, but it can often evaluate plan design, provider network, funding structure, and whether alternative arrangements such as level-funded coverage deserve comparison.
