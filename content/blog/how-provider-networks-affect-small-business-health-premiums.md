When small business owners compare health insurance plans, they usually start with the obvious numbers: the monthly premium, the deductible, the copays, and the out-of-pocket maximum. That is understandable. Those numbers are easy to see.

But one of the most important cost drivers is not always as obvious. It is the provider network.

A provider network is the group of doctors, hospitals, clinics, specialists, labs, and other healthcare providers that have contracts with the insurance carrier. When employees stay in network, they usually pay less. When they go out of network, they may pay more, or they may have limited coverage except in emergencies.

Most employees think about the network in simple terms: “Can I keep my doctor?” or “Is my hospital covered?” Business owners need to look at it one step deeper.

The network does not just affect access. It also affects cost. CMS rate-review instructions specifically identify a plan’s provider network, delivery system characteristics, and utilization management practices as factors that may affect plan-level pricing. In plain English, the network can influence what the insurance company expects to pay for healthcare.

That means two plans can look similar on the surface but still have very different pricing because the underlying networks are different.

For small businesses, this is an important part of the renewal conversation. A broader network may give employees more freedom. A smaller or more focused network may help reduce premiums. The best choice depends on the company, the employees, and the employer’s overall benefits strategy.

#### A Network Is Not Just a List of Doctors

It is easy to think of a provider network as a directory. From the employee’s perspective, that is how it often feels. They want to know if their primary care doctor is covered, whether their child’s pediatrician is in network, and whether they can still use the hospital system they know.

Those questions matter. If employees cannot use the plan easily, they may not value it, even if the premium is lower.

But from the insurance company’s perspective, the network is also a pricing tool. The carrier looks at which hospitals, doctors, and facilities are included in the network. It also looks at the prices it has negotiated with those providers. A plan that includes more expensive hospital systems may have higher expected claims. A plan that steers care toward lower-cost providers may have lower expected claims. That does not mean the lower-cost network is always better. It means the network is one of the reasons one plan may cost more than another.

This is why a small business should not compare health plans only by premium and deductible. The network may be doing a lot of work behind the scenes.

#### Why Broad Networks Often Cost More

A broad network usually gives employees more choice. It may include more hospital systems, more physician groups, more specialists, and more out-of-network flexibility. For employees, that can feel safer and more convenient.

For example, a small engineering firm in the Chicago suburbs may have employees spread across several counties. A broad PPO network can make it easier for employees to keep their doctors and avoid disruption. A Texas technology company with employees in Austin, Dallas, Houston, and San Antonio may also value a broad network because employees are not all using the same local medical system.

That flexibility can be worth paying for but, broader access often comes with a higher price. If a network includes expensive hospitals and high-cost provider systems, the insurance carrier expects higher claims. Those expected claims can show up in the premium.

PPOs remain the most common employer health plan type. KFF’s 2025 Employer Health Benefits Survey found that 46% of covered workers were enrolled in PPOs. KFF also explains that PPOs allow both in-network and out-of-network care, usually with lower cost-sharing when employees stay in network.

That flexibility is part of why employees often like PPOs. It is also one reason employers need to ask whether the extra access is worth the extra cost.

#### Why Narrow or Focused Networks Can Lower Premiums

A narrow network limits the number of participating providers.

At first, that may sound like a bad thing. Sometimes it is. If the network does not include the doctors and hospitals employees need, the plan can create frustration quickly. But a focused network can also be a legitimate cost-control tool.

The basic idea is simple: instead of giving employees access to almost every provider, the plan directs employees toward a smaller group of providers that may have better pricing, better coordination, or more predictable costs.

KFF describes narrow network plans as plans that restrict the number of participating providers in order to reduce costs. In 2025, 9% of firms offering health benefits offered a narrow network plan. A narrower network may work well when employees live in the same general area and the network includes the providers they already use. It may also work when the premium savings are meaningful enough to make coverage more affordable for both the business and employees.

But this is where small businesses need to be careful. A narrow network that saves money but causes employees to lose important doctors may not be a good tradeoff. A lower premium can still be a bad value if the plan becomes harder to use.

#### The Price of Healthcare Can Vary Widely

Provider networks matter because healthcare prices are not the same everywhere.

The same general service can cost very different amounts depending on the hospital, facility, or provider group. RAND’s employer price transparency study found that in 2022, employers and private insurers paid hospitals an average of 254% of what Medicare would have paid for the same inpatient and outpatient services at the same facilities.

That is a big gap, and it helps explain why networks matter. If a plan includes high-cost providers and employees often use them, the cost of claims can be higher. If a plan has stronger contracts with lower-cost providers, expected claims may be lower. This does not mean one hospital is automatically better or worse than another. It means the price of healthcare is not uniform.

Small business owners understand this in almost every other area of business. Two law firms may charge different rates. Two software vendors may offer different pricing. Two contractors may quote different amounts for the same job.

Healthcare works the same way, except the prices are much harder to see.

#### PPO, HMO, EPO, and POS: What Employers Should Understand

The labels can get confusing, but the basic idea is manageable.

A PPO usually gives employees more flexibility. They can use in-network providers at a lower cost and may have some coverage for out-of-network providers. This is one reason PPOs are popular, but that flexibility often comes with higher premiums.

An HMO is usually more controlled. Employees generally need to use the plan’s network, and out-of-network care is often not covered except in emergencies. That can help control costs, but employees may feel restricted if they are used to broader access. KFF defines HMOs as plans that do not cover non-emergency services provided out of network.

An EPO is also network-focused. Employees generally need to stay in network, except for emergencies. It may not always feel as managed as an HMO, but employees still need to be careful about which providers are included.

A POS plan is more of a hybrid. It may include features of both HMO and PPO designs.

The key point is this: the label does not tell the whole story. One PPO may have a better local network than another PPO. One HMO may be perfectly workable if the network includes the doctors and hospitals employees already use.

The right question is not simply, “Is this a PPO or an HMO?” The better question is: Does this network fit our employees well enough to justify the cost?

#### The Employee Disruption Problem

Network changes can look great in a spreadsheet and still cause problems in real life.

This is especially true for small businesses. In a 15-person or 25-person company, a few unhappy employees can create a lot of friction. If someone loses access to a child’s specialist, a trusted therapist, an OB/GYN, or a hospital system they rely on, the issue becomes personal very quickly. That does not mean employers should avoid every network change. It means they should review the tradeoff before making a decision.

A business owner should think about where employees actually live, not just where the business is located. A company based in one part of Chicago may have employees commuting from several suburbs. A Texas employer may have workers spread across multiple metro areas. A plan that looks strong near the office may not work as well near employees’ homes.

This is one reason employee ZIP codes matter during a plan review. The network needs to work where people actually seek care.

#### Mental Health Access Should Not Be an Afterthought

Many employers review hospitals and primary care doctors first. That makes sense, but mental health access deserves special attention.

KFF’s 2025 survey found that employers were more confident about timely access to primary care and specialty care than mental health care. Among firms offering health benefits, 92% believed their largest plan had enough providers for timely primary care access, and 89% believed it had enough for specialty care. Only 70% believed it had enough mental health providers for timely access. That gap matters.

A network may look fine on paper but still be weak for behavioral health. For employees dealing with stress, anxiety, depression, family issues, or substance-use concerns, “covered” does not always mean “easy to access.” For small employers that care about retention, productivity, and employee well-being, mental health access is not just a nice extra. It can be an important part of plan quality.

#### The Two-Option Strategy: A Practical Way to Use Networks

A small business does not always have to choose one network for everyone.

In some fully insured small-group arrangements, an employer may be able to offer more than one plan option. That can create a useful cost-containment strategy without forcing every employee into the same tradeoff. For example, a company might offer a lower-cost HMO or focused-network plan as the main option. The employer could make that plan very affordable by contributing more heavily toward it.

At the same time, the company might also offer a broader PPO plan for employees who want more provider choice. Those employees would still have access to the broader network, but they would pay more of the added cost themselves. This can be a smart middle ground.

The company is not saying, “Everyone has to take the smaller network.” It is saying: “We will make the cost-effective plan very affordable. If you want broader access, you can buy up.”

That approach can help the company manage its budget while still respecting employee choice. This strategy works best when the lower-cost plan is genuinely usable. If the HMO or focused network includes the providers employees already use, the plan may be attractive. If it is too restrictive, employees may see it as a downgrade.

Carrier rules, contribution requirements, participation rules, and plan availability also matter. This is not something to set up casually. But when it is available and communicated clearly, it can be a better solution than choosing one plan and hoping it works for everyone.

#### A Simple Doctor Match Review Can Reduce Guesswork

Before assuming employees will reject a lower-cost HMO or focused network, a small employer can do a simple doctor match review. The idea is straightforward: ask employees whether there are any doctors, hospitals, pediatricians, specialists, or mental health providers that are especially important for them or their families to keep in network. The employer does not need to ask about medical conditions, prescriptions, diagnoses, or treatment history. The review should focus only on provider names and locations.

This can be a real advantage for small businesses. A large employer with hundreds or thousands of employees usually cannot check every employee’s preferred providers before changing plans. A small employer with 5 to 50 employees may be able to do a more practical review. If many of the important providers already participate in the lower-cost HMO or focused network, the employer may be able to offer that plan with more confidence. If many important providers are missing, the employer may decide the savings are not worth the disruption.

This is a good example of why network strategy should not be based on assumptions. Sometimes a lower-cost network is more workable than employees expect. Other times, the provider disruption is too significant. The point is to check before making the decision.

#### How Networks Fit With Fully Insured and Level-Funded Plans

Provider networks matter in both fully insured and level-funded plans. In a fully insured plan, the carrier bundles the network, claims risk, administration, and premium into one product. The employer chooses from the options available in that market.

In a level-funded plan, the employer may see more of the cost structure, including expected claims funding, administrative costs, and stop-loss protection. But the network still matters because it influences what claims may cost. A level-funded plan with a poor network fit can create employee frustration. A fully insured plan with an expensive broad network can create unnecessary cost. The funding method matters, but it does not replace the need to review the network carefully.

This is why a good renewal review should look at more than the premium. It should also ask whether the network, plan design, funding structure, and employer contribution strategy fit together.

#### When a Smaller Network May Make Sense

A smaller or more focused network may make sense when the employees are mostly local, the network includes the providers they already use, and the premium savings are meaningful. This can be especially useful for a small business trying to keep employee contributions affordable. If the lower-cost network is strong in the area, employees may prefer a plan that costs them less each paycheck, even if it gives them fewer provider choices.

But the employer should not assume employees will accept the tradeoff automatically. A network change should be explained clearly. Employees should understand what is changing, which providers are included, and what they need to do before scheduling care.

The goal is not just to reduce premium. The goal is to reduce cost without making the plan feel unusable.

#### When a Broad Network May Be Worth Paying For

A broader network may be worth the cost when employees are spread across multiple cities or states, when they have established specialist relationships, or when the employer is trying to compete for talent against larger companies.

Some employers also choose broader networks simply to reduce disruption. That can be a reasonable decision. If the premium difference is manageable and employees highly value choice, a broad PPO-style network may be worth paying for.

The mistake is not choosing a broad network. The mistake is choosing it automatically without asking whether the company is paying for access employees actually need.

#### A Better Way to Review the Network

A practical network review should start with real employee needs.

Are the major local hospitals included? Are common physician groups included? Does the plan work near employee home ZIP codes? Are mental health providers available? Are employees likely to lose doctors? Is there out-of-network coverage? Are there referral rules? Could the employer offer both a lower-cost option and a broader buy-up option?

Those questions are more useful than simply asking which plan has the lowest premium. A good plan review should help the business owner understand the tradeoff:

How much are we saving, and what access are employees giving up?

Sometimes the savings are worth it. Sometimes they are not. The value comes from making that decision intentionally.

#### The Bottom Line

Provider networks are one of the most important hidden drivers of small business health insurance premiums. A broader network may give employees more flexibility, but it can also increase expected claims costs. A narrower or tiered network may reduce premiums, but it can create access issues if the fit is poor.

For small businesses in Illinois and Texas, the best answer is not automatically “broad network” or “narrow network.” The best answer depends on where employees live, which providers they use, how much disruption they can tolerate, and how the company wants to structure its contribution.

In some cases, the smartest strategy may be to offer both: a lower-cost network plan with a stronger employer contribution, and a broader network option for employees who want more choice and are willing to pay more. A smart health plan review should always ask: Are we paying for the right network — or just the familiar one?

#### FAQ SECTION

\1. How do provider networks affect health insurance premiums?

Provider networks affect premiums because they influence what the health plan expects to pay for care. CMS rate-review instructions identify provider network, delivery system characteristics, and utilization management practices as factors that may affect plan-level pricing.

\2. Are PPO plans usually more expensive than HMO or EPO plans?

PPO plans often cost more because they usually offer broader access and out-of-network flexibility. PPOs are also the most common employer plan type, with 46% of covered workers enrolled in PPOs in KFF’s 2025 Employer Health Benefits Survey.

\3. What is a narrow network health plan?

A narrow network plan limits the number of participating providers, usually to help reduce costs. KFF reported that 9% of firms offering health benefits offered a narrow network plan in 2025.

\4. Is a narrow network always bad for employees?

No. A narrow network can work well if it includes the doctors, hospitals, and specialists employees actually use. It becomes a problem when employees lose access to important providers or have trouble finding care.

\5. Can a small business offer both an HMO and a PPO?

In some fully insured small-group arrangements, an employer may be able to offer more than one plan option, such as a lower-cost HMO and a broader PPO. Carrier rules, contribution requirements, participation requirements, and plan availability should be reviewed before setting this up.

\6. Why does mental health access matter in a provider network?

A plan can have strong hospital and primary care access but still have limited behavioral health access. KFF’s 2025 survey found employers were less confident about timely mental health access than primary or specialty care access.

\7. Should small businesses always choose the cheapest network?

No. The cheapest plan may create employee disruption if the network is too restrictive. Employers should compare premium savings against provider access, employee expectations, and disruption risk.
