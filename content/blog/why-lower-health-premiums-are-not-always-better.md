When a small business gets a health insurance renewal, the first number everyone looks at is the premium.

The premium is easy to compare. One plan costs $8,000 per month. Another costs $7,200. A third costs $6,900. When health insurance is already one of the company’s biggest expenses, the lower number gets attention fast. But, a lower premium does not always mean a better plan.

Sometimes the lower-cost plan is a smart move. Other times, the lower premium is hiding a tradeoff that will show up later through higher employee costs, a weaker provider network, prescription drug problems, more administrative work, or a plan structure that is not a good fit for the company.

For small businesses, the goal should not be to buy the cheapest health plan. The goal should be to buy the plan that gives the best balance of cost, employee access, risk protection, and long-term fit. That requires looking past the premium.

#### Why the Lowest Premium Is So Tempting

Small employers are under real pressure.

KFF’s 2025 Employer Health Benefits Survey found that the average annual premium for employer-sponsored family coverage reached $26,993 in 2025. The same survey reported an average annual deductible of $1,886 for single coverage among covered workers in plans with a general annual deductible. Those numbers help explain why owners focus so heavily on cost.

If a lower-premium plan appears to save the company thousands of dollars per year, it deserves attention. The mistake is assuming that lower premium automatically means better value. Health insurance cost can move from one place to another. A plan may lower the monthly premium by increasing deductibles, limiting the network, changing prescription drug coverage, increasing employee cost-sharing, or shifting more risk to the employer.

That may still be acceptable. But the business owner should understand the tradeoff before making the change.

#### Premium Is Only One Part of the Cost

A health plan has several different cost layers.

The employer pays premium. Employees may pay part of the premium through payroll deductions. Employees also pay out-of-pocket costs when they use care, such as deductibles, copays, and coinsurance. Healthcare.gov explains that out-of-pocket costs include deductibles, coinsurance, copayments, and costs for services not covered by the plan.

That means a plan with a lower premium may still cost employees more when they actually use healthcare. A deductible is the amount a person pays for covered healthcare services before the plan starts paying. Healthcare.gov gives the example of a $2,000 deductible, where the person pays the first $2,000 of covered services before the plan begins paying according to the plan rules. For a business owner, this matters because employees often judge the plan by what happens when they need care. A lower premium may help the company budget, but employees may feel worse off if they face higher costs at the doctor, hospital, pharmacy, or specialist.

#### A Cheap Plan Can Become Expensive for Employees

A lower-premium plan may look good in the employer budget but create problems for employees. For example, a company may move from a plan with a $1,500 deductible to a plan with a $5,000 deductible. The premium goes down. The employer saves money. But employees who use the plan may now feel like the coverage does not help until they spend thousands of dollars. That can hurt morale.

This is especially important for small businesses because benefits are personal. In a large company, employees may complain to HR. In a small company, they may complain directly to the owner, office manager, or supervisor. The lower-cost plan may still be the right choice. But the employer should know whether the savings come from real efficiency or from shifting more cost to employees.

#### The Network May Be Smaller

Provider network is one of the biggest places where lower premiums can hide tradeoffs. A plan may cost less because it uses a smaller network, an HMO, an EPO, or a more focused group of doctors and hospitals. That can be a good cost-control strategy if the network fits the employees. But if employees lose access to their doctors, hospitals, specialists, pediatricians, or mental health providers, the lower premium may create frustration quickly.

This is not a reason to avoid smaller networks. In fact, a smaller network can be a smart choice if it includes the providers employees actually use. It may even be possible to offer a lower-cost HMO as the base plan and a broader PPO as a buy-up option.

A small employer should check whether the lower-cost network works where employees live and seek care. A doctor match review can help. That means checking whether employees’ important providers are included in the lower-cost network before making the switch.

For small employers, this can be a real advantage. A 20-person company may be able to do a practical provider check in a way a 2,000-person company cannot.

#### Prescription Drug Coverage May Change

Prescription drug coverage is another area where a lower-premium plan may create problems. Two plans may both say they cover prescriptions. But that does not mean they cover them the same way. One plan may have lower copays for generics but higher costs for brand-name drugs. Another may place more medications on higher tiers. Another may require prior authorization before certain drugs are approved. Specialty drugs may have separate rules or cost-sharing.

KFF’s 2025 Employer Health Benefits Survey notes that employer plans commonly use prescription drug formularies, cost-sharing tiers, and other tools to manage prescription coverage. For employees who take only low-cost generics, the change may not matter much. For employees using brand-name drugs, specialty drugs, or medications with special approval rules, the difference can be significant.

A small employer usually does not need to become a pharmacy expert. But before choosing a lower-premium plan, someone should review how the prescription drug coverage changes. Otherwise, the company may save money on premium and create a new problem at the pharmacy counter.

#### The Out-of-Pocket Maximum Matters

The out-of-pocket maximum is another important number. Healthcare.gov defines the out-of-pocket maximum as the most a person has to pay for covered services in a plan year. For 2026 Marketplace plans, Healthcare.gov lists the maximum as $10,600 for an individual and $21,200 for a family.

Employer plans can vary, but the concept matters in every plan comparison. A lower-premium plan may come with a higher out-of-pocket maximum. That may not matter to an employee who barely uses care. It can matter a lot to an employee who has surgery, a serious illness, a complicated pregnancy, cancer treatment, or a major accident.

The employer should understand the worst-case employee exposure before choosing a cheaper plan. A plan is not automatically bad because the out-of-pocket maximum is higher. But the employer should know who is carrying more of the risk.

#### Lower Premium Can Mean More Employee Confusion

Cheaper plans are sometimes more complicated. That may happen because of network rules, referral rules, prior authorization, pharmacy restrictions, separate specialty drug rules, or unfamiliar plan structures. A broad PPO may be expensive, but employees usually understand it. A lower-cost HMO, EPO, reference-based pricing plan, or level-funded plan may require more explanation.

That does not make those options wrong. Some of them can be very good. But if employees do not understand how to use the plan, the employer may face problems. Employees may go out of network by mistake, skip care, get surprised by bills, or blame the company when the issue is really plan design.

A lower premium is not worth much if employees cannot figure out how to use the coverage.

#### Fully Insured vs. Level-Funded: Cheaper Quotes Need a Careful Review

Level-funded plans can be attractive for small employers, especially groups with favorable demographics or lower expected claims.

They may offer a lower monthly cost, more claims visibility, and sometimes the possibility of a surplus refund if claims come in lower than expected. KFF describes level-funded arrangements as self-funded-style plans packaged with stop-loss coverage, and KFF reported that in 2025, 37% of covered workers in firms with 10 to 199 workers were enrolled in a level-funded plan. That is important. Level funding is no longer some obscure concept. It is a major part of the small-employer market.

But a lower level-funded quote should still be reviewed carefully. The employer should understand the network, prescription drug program, stop-loss protection, maximum claim exposure, renewal process, surplus refund rules, and what happens if claims are higher than expected.

A level-funded plan may be the better choice. It may also be the wrong fit if the employer wants maximum simplicity or cannot handle the added complexity. A lower quote is only useful if the structure behind it makes sense.

#### A Simple Example

Imagine a 24-person contractor in Texas.

The company’s current health plan renews at $18,000 per month. A new plan is offered at $15,500 per month. On the surface, the savings look excellent.

But after reviewing the details, the employer learns that the lower-cost plan has a smaller network, higher prescription drug costs for some brand-name medications, and a higher out-of-pocket maximum.

That does not automatically make the cheaper plan bad. It may still be the right move if employees’ doctors are in network, most employees use low-cost prescriptions, and the company communicates the changes clearly. But if several employees lose important providers and a few face much higher drug costs, the cheaper plan may create more problems than it solves. The premium savings need to be compared against the full impact.

#### When the Lower-Cost Plan May Be the Right Choice

Sometimes the cheaper plan really is the better plan. That may happen when the lower premium comes from a more efficient network, a smarter funding structure, better plan design, or a contribution strategy that fits the workforce.

For example, a company may offer a lower-cost HMO as the base plan and contribute heavily toward it. Employees who want broader access can buy up to a PPO. That can lower company costs while still giving employees a choice. A lower-premium plan may also work well when employees are young, local, comfortable with the network, and not heavily tied to specific providers.

The point is not to avoid cheap plans. The point is to understand why the plan is cheaper.

#### When the Lower-Cost Plan May Be a Problem

A lower-cost plan deserves more caution when the savings come mostly from shifting risk or disruption to employees. Warning signs include a much smaller network, major increases in deductibles or out-of-pocket maximums, weaker prescription drug coverage, unclear specialty drug rules, no good employee support, or plan terms the employer does not fully understand.

Another warning sign is speed. If the employer is making the decision at the last minute, there may not be enough time to review provider networks, drug coverage, payroll deductions, employee communication, or enrollment setup. A cheap plan chosen in a rush can become expensive in employee frustration.

#### How Small Employers Should Compare Plans

A good comparison should look beyond premium. The employer should review the network, employee providers, deductibles, copays, coinsurance, out-of-pocket maximums, prescription drug rules, contribution strategy, funding structure, and likely employee disruption.

The employer should also ask how the plan will feel to employees. A plan that looks strong on paper may fail if employees see it as confusing or unusable. This is where a benefits review becomes valuable. The goal is not just to quote plans. The goal is to compare tradeoffs in a way the business owner can actually use.

A cheaper plan may be better. A more expensive plan may be worth keeping. A two-option strategy may be better than either one by itself.

#### The Bottom Line

A lower health insurance premium can be attractive, and sometimes it is the right move. But, lower premium does not automatically mean better value.

The savings may come from a smaller network, higher employee cost-sharing, prescription drug changes, more plan complexity, or a funding structure that needs closer review.

For small businesses, the better approach is to compare the full plan, not just the rate.

A smart health insurance review should ask: What are we saving, what are we giving up, and will employees still be able to use the plan effectively?

That is the difference between buying the cheapest plan and building a better benefits strategy.

#### FAQ SECTION

\1. Is the cheapest health insurance plan usually the best choice for a small business?

Not always. A lower premium may come with a smaller network, higher deductibles, higher out-of-pocket costs, prescription drug restrictions, or more employee confusion.

\2. Why can a lower-premium plan cost employees more?

A lower-premium plan may shift more cost to employees through deductibles, copays, coinsurance, or higher out-of-pocket maximums. Healthcare.gov explains that out-of-pocket costs include deductibles, copayments, and coinsurance for covered services.

\3. What is a deductible?

A deductible is the amount a person pays for covered healthcare services before the plan starts to pay. Healthcare.gov gives the example of a $2,000 deductible, where the person pays the first $2,000 of covered services before the plan begins paying according to the plan rules.

\4. Why does the provider network matter when comparing plans?

The network affects which doctors, hospitals, and specialists employees can use. A lower-cost plan may have a smaller network, which can save money but may also create disruption if employees lose access to important providers.

\5. Can prescription drug coverage be different between health plans?

Yes. Plans may use different drug tiers, formularies, prior authorization rules, preferred pharmacies, and specialty drug rules. KFF notes that employer plans commonly use formularies and cost-sharing tiers for prescription drugs.

\6. Are level-funded plans always cheaper than fully insured plans?

No. Level-funded plans may be attractive for some small employers, but the employer should review the network, claims funding, stop-loss protection, prescription drug program, renewal process, and plan terms before deciding.

\7. What should a small employer compare besides premium?

A good comparison should include provider network, employee provider access, deductibles, copays, coinsurance, out-of-pocket maximums, prescription coverage, employer contribution strategy, employee disruption, and funding structure.
