When a small business owner looks at a health insurance renewal, the natural question is: “Why is our rate going up?”

Many owners assume the answer must be tied directly to their own employees’ medical claims. If the group had a good year, they expect a modest renewal. If the group had a bad year, they expect a larger increase. That logic makes sense. But for many fully insured ACA-compliant small-group health plans, it is not exactly how pricing works.

In the ACA small-group market, insurers generally cannot base premiums on a company’s employees’ health status, medical history, gender, or prior claims experience. Instead, premiums are primarily shaped by a narrower set of permitted rating factors, including age, geographic area, tobacco use and family enrollment. That rating system creates more stability and better access for many small employers. But it can also leave business owners confused, especially when their own workforce seems healthier than average yet their premiums remain high.

Understanding how these rates are built is one of the most important first steps in deciding whether your current health plan still makes sense.

#### What “Small Group” Usually Means

In most states, the ACA small-group market generally applies to employers with 1 to 50 full-time and full-time-equivalent employees, although there are some state-specific differences. For many small businesses in Illinois and Texas (our primary areas of operation), this is the market they are dealing with when they shop for a traditional fully insured group health plan.

This matters because small-group rules are different from large-group rules. Larger employers may have pricing and underwriting structures that look more directly at the group’s own experience. Small employers buying ACA-compliant fully insured coverage operate within a more standardized rating framework.

#### Before the ACA, Insurers Had More Ways to Price Small Groups

Before the ACA’s market reforms took effect, small-group health insurance underwriting could consider a wider range of factors. Depending on the state and carrier, insurers might look at health conditions, claims history, gender mix, industry, or other characteristics when determining rates. CMS has noted that before 2014, small employer rates could be affected by health status, claims history, gender, occupation, and small employer size.

That old system created major problems.

A small business with one employee facing cancer treatment, a major surgery, or an expensive chronic condition could face steep pricing pressure. Employers with older workforces or employees with known medical issues could also have a harder time finding affordable coverage. The ACA changed that by moving the small-group market toward a more standardized structure. The goal was to make coverage more accessible and prevent insurers from heavily penalizing certain groups based on health conditions or employee demographics.

#### The Main Factors That Can Affect ACA Small Group Premiums

For ACA-compliant individual and small-group coverage, the permitted rating factors are limited. The most important ones are:

\1. Age - Older individuals can be charged more than younger individuals, but the difference is capped. Under federal ACA rules, adult premium variation by age is generally limited to a 3-to-1 ratio, meaning an older adult’s premium cannot be more than three times the premium charged to a younger adult for the same plan and rating area.

\2. Geographic Area - Where employees live can affect rates. Healthcare costs, provider prices, local competition, and regional market conditions can differ meaningfully from one area to another. That means a small business in Chicago may see different pricing than a similar business in a smaller Illinois market, and a company in Dallas may see different results than one in rural Texas.

\3. Tobacco Use - Insurers may charge more for tobacco users, subject to applicable federal and state rules. Healthcare.gov notes that insurers can charge tobacco users up to 50% more in certain markets, although states may impose different limits.

\4. Family Enrollment - Premiums can be higher when coverage includes a spouse or dependents. The total cost of coverage naturally increases as more family members are enrolled.

These factors may sound straightforward, but they explain only part of what business owners feel at renewal time. The biggest surprise often comes from understanding what does not directly affect ACA small-group premiums.

#### What Cannot Directly Affect ACA Small Group Premiums

For ACA-compliant small-group health insurance, insurers generally cannot directly vary rates based on:

- Health status
- Pre-existing conditions
- Prior claims experience
- Gender
- Occupation
- How long someone has held coverage
- The small employer’s size within the small-group market

CMS specifically identified those factors as no longer permitted for premium rating in the small-employer market under the ACA reforms. Healthcare.gov also states that insurers cannot charge men and women different prices for the same plan and cannot take current health or medical history into account when setting premiums.

This is a major shift from how many business owners intuitively think insurance should work.

A group with low claims does not automatically get a low fully insured ACA rate. A group with higher claims is not necessarily singled out for a claims-based renewal in the same way a larger experience-rated group might be. Instead, the employer is participating in a broader small-group market structure.

#### Why the 3-to-1 Age Rule Matters

The 3-to-1 age rating limit is one of the most important concepts for business owners to understand. In plain English, it means that insurers cannot price older adults at more than three times the rate charged to younger adults for the same plan and area.

That rule is meant to prevent older individuals and groups with older employees from being priced out of coverage. But it also means younger employees bear more of the cost than they would under a pricing model that followed expected medical expenses more closely. This becomes especially important for certain small businesses.

A technology firm in Austin, an engineering firm in suburban Chicago, or a contracting company with a younger field workforce may feel that its workforce demographics should produce especially favorable pricing. Yet in the fully insured ACA small-group market, that advantage is only partly reflected through age rating. Other favorable traits, such as lower expected claims or fewer known health conditions, generally cannot be used directly in the premium.

That is one reason some younger, healthier employers feel disconnected from the rates they receive.

#### Why Your Own Claims May Not Explain Your Renewal

Small business owners often focus heavily on their own group’s recent claims. That is understandable. Claims feel concrete and it is how other lines of insurance price their renewals. If employees barely used the plan, an owner may expect a low renewal.

But in the ACA fully insured small-group market, your own claims history is generally not the direct pricing engine. Instead, renewal pricing is influenced by the broader rating structure, the carrier’s approved rate changes, the plan selected, the ages and coverage tiers of those enrolled, and the applicable geographic rating area.

That does not mean claims are irrelevant to the overall health insurance system. Carriers still consider broader market experience when pricing products and filing rates. But at the individual small-group employer level, your specific claims are not usually used the same way many owners assume.

This distinction helps explain a frustrating experience many businesses have: “We barely used the plan, but the renewal still increased.” In a fully insured ACA small-group framework, that can happen without anything being wrong.

#### A Simple Example

Imagine two small employers:

Company A: 16-person engineering firm with: Mostly younger employees, strong participation, few dependents enrolled, little known recent medical activity

Company B: 16-person professional services firm with: Older average employee ages, more family coverage, higher expected healthcare needs

Under ACA small-group rating rules, the rates will not be identical. Age and enrollment structure matter. But Company A’s full health-risk advantage cannot simply be priced directly into the premium, because the insurer generally cannot rate the group based on health status or claims history.

This is where some business owners begin to ask whether a different funding structure, such as a level-funded plan, may be worth comparing.

#### Where Level-Funded Plans Enter the Conversation

A level-funded health plan is different from a traditional fully insured ACA small-group plan. While the details vary by carrier and state, level-funded arrangements typically combine:

- A fixed monthly payment
- Administrative costs
- Claims funding
- Stop-loss protection against unusually high claims

For some employers, especially those with favorable demographics or lower expected claims, level-funded proposals may offer a pricing approach that pays closer attention to the employer’s own risk profile. That is why these plans often come up when a younger or healthier small business begins questioning fully insured ACA rates.

Still, this needs to be said plainly: Level-funded plans are not automatically cheaper nor automatically better.

A level-funded option may look attractive for one small business and not for another. The outcome can depend on the group’s size, employee census, plan design, participation, medical underwriting process where applicable, and the employer’s comfort with the structure being offered.

The smart move is not to assume one funding method is superior. The smart move is to compare them intelligently.

#### Why This Matters for Illinois and Texas Small Businesses

Small employers in Illinois and Texas are often trying to balance three goals at once:

- Keep benefit costs manageable
- Offer something strong enough to recruit and retain employees
- Avoid unnecessary complexity

Understanding ACA small-group rating rules helps owners make better decisions on all three fronts. If a business has an older workforce, significant medical needs, or simply wants the familiar structure of a traditional fully insured plan, ACA small-group coverage may remain a strong fit.

If a business has a younger or more favorable demographic profile, it may be worth reviewing whether the current rate structure is still the best match. Either way, better understanding leads to better decisions.

#### What Business Owners Should Review Before Accepting a Renewal

Before simply accepting the next health insurance renewal, a small business owner should understand:

- How much of the increase is tied to age changes or plan changes
- Whether the current plan design still fits employees’ needs
- Whether employee contributions remain sustainable
- Whether fully insured alternatives have been compared
- Whether a level-funded option is worth evaluating
- Whether the current benefit package supports hiring and retention goals

These are not just insurance questions. They are business planning questions. For a small company, even a moderate health plan inefficiency can affect cash flow, compensation decisions, and the ability to compete for talent. And, just the simple act of exploring a level-funded plan (whether you actually move forward with it or not) can provide you with information that shapes your fully-insured plan design as well as strategic HR decisions surrounding your employees.

#### The Bottom Line

ACA small-group health insurance rates are built around a standardized rating structure. Premiums can generally vary based on age, geography, tobacco use, and family enrollment, but not directly on health status, gender, or prior claims experience.

That framework created important protections and made the small-group market more accessible. But it also means some employers, especially younger and healthier groups, may feel that their rates do not fully reflect their own workforce profile.

For small businesses in Illinois and Texas, understanding how rates are calculated is the first step toward deciding whether the current plan is still the right fit — or whether it is time to compare other options.

#### FAQ SECTION

\1. How are small business health insurance rates calculated under the ACA?

ACA-compliant small-group premiums are generally based on age, geographic area, tobacco use, and family enrollment. Insurers generally cannot directly base premiums on health status, gender, or prior claims history.

\2. Do my employees’ claims affect our fully insured small-group renewal?

For ACA-compliant fully insured small-group plans, an employer’s own claims history is generally not used directly in the same way it may be for larger experience-rated groups. Broader market pricing, plan design, enrolled ages, and rating area still matter.

\3. Can insurers charge older employees more than younger employees?

Yes, but adult age-based pricing is generally limited to a 3-to-1 ratio under federal ACA rules, meaning the oldest adult rate cannot exceed three times the youngest adult rate for the same plan and rating area.

\4. Can men and women be charged different prices for the same ACA small-group plan?

No. Healthcare.gov states that insurers cannot charge men and women different prices for the same plan.

\5. Can pre-existing conditions increase a small business’s ACA group premium?

ACA-compliant plans cannot price coverage based on an individual’s current health or medical history in the small-group market.

\6. Why would a younger, healthier company compare level-funded plans?

Because fully insured ACA small-group rates generally do not directly reflect the group’s own health status or claims history. Some employers with favorable demographics want to compare whether a different funding structure may be a better fit.

\7. Are level-funded plans always better than fully insured ACA plans?

No. Level-funded plans can be worth comparing, but the right fit depends on the employer’s size, employee profile, plan design, participation, and risk tolerance.

\8. What should I review before renewing my small business health plan?

Review the renewal increase, employee demographics, plan design, contribution strategy, alternative fully insured plans, and whether a level-funded comparison makes sense for your business.
