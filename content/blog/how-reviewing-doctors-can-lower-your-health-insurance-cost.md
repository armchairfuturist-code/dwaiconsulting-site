Many small business owners assume employees will reject an HMO or smaller provider network. Sometimes they will but, sometimes they will not.

Employees usually do not care about having the biggest network in theory. They care about something more personal: whether their doctors, hospitals, pediatricians, specialists, therapists, and local medical groups are included. That creates a practical opportunity for small employers.

Before rejecting a lower-cost HMO or focused-network plan, a small business can do a simple doctor match review. The goal is to compare the providers employees actually care about against the network in the lower-cost plan. This is one of the few areas where being small can be an advantage. A company with 20 employees may be able to review provider fit in a way a company with 2,000 employees usually cannot. Large employers often need broad assumptions. Small employers can sometimes be more precise.

The point is not to ask employees private medical questions. The employer does not need to know anyone’s diagnosis, prescription, treatment history, or medical condition. The review should focus only on provider names and locations. Done correctly, a doctor match review can help answer a useful renewal question: Could we move to a lower-cost network without creating major provider disruption?

#### What Is a Doctor Match Review?

A doctor match review is a simple comparison between two lists. The first list is the providers employees want to keep using. The second list is the providers included in the lower-cost health plan network.

For example, a company may currently offer a broad PPO plan. At renewal, the employer receives a quote for a lower-cost HMO. Instead of assuming the HMO will not work, the employer asks: “Are the doctors employees already use included in this network?”

If the answer is mostly yes, the lower-cost plan may be more realistic than expected. If the answer is mostly no, the employer may decide the savings are not worth the disruption.

This is not a perfect process. Provider directories can be wrong or outdated. CMS notes that provider directories are not always accurate and recommends contacting the insurance company when network status is unclear. But even with that limitation, a doctor match review is better than guessing.

#### Why This Can Affect Health Insurance Costs

Provider networks are not just access tools. They can affect plan pricing. CMS rate-review instructions allow plan-level adjustments for a plan’s provider network, delivery system characteristics, and utilization management practices. In plain English, the network can affect what the carrier expects to pay for care.

That is why a broad PPO may cost more than a more focused HMO or EPO. The broader plan may include more providers, more hospital systems, and more flexibility.

That flexibility can be valuable. KFF’s 2025 Employer Health Benefits Survey found that PPOs remain the most common employer plan type, covering 46% of covered workers. KFF also describes narrow networks as plans that limit participating providers to reduce costs, with 9% of firms offering health benefits offering a narrow network plan in 2025. This begs the question: Are we paying extra for provider access our employees actually need?

#### Why Small Employers Have an Advantage

Large employers have more data and more resources. But small employers often know their workforce better. A 15-person company may know that most employees live in the same few ZIP codes. A 25-person firm may know that several employees use the same local hospital system. A 40-person contractor may know that affordability matters more to employees than broad national access. That practical knowledge matters.

A large employer usually cannot manually check every employee’s doctors before changing plans. A small employer often can do a lighter, more personal review which could save everyone a lot of money

#### How to Ask Employees Without Asking Medical Questions

The review should be simple and respectful. The employer should not ask why someone sees a doctor. It should not ask about conditions, medications, procedures, or diagnoses. The way to ask is: “If we review lower-cost health plan options, are there any doctors, hospitals, specialists, pediatricians, therapists, or medical groups that are important for you or your family to keep in network?”

Employees can provide provider names and locations. The location matters because a doctor may participate at one office but not another. The exact plan name also matters because a provider may accept one carrier network but not another. The employer or advisor can then compare those providers against the lower-cost network being considered. You could also simply provide the network link to the employees and ask them to verify their preferred providers. This keeps the process focused on access, not private health information.

#### Why the Results Matter

The results of a doctor match review can change the renewal conversation. If most important providers are already included in the lower-cost network, the employer may be able to offer that plan with more confidence. If many important providers are missing, the employer may decide the savings are not worth the frustration.

The review can also support a two-option strategy.

For example, the employer might offer a lower-cost HMO as the base plan and make it very affordable through a stronger company contribution. Employees who want broader access could still choose a PPO buy-up option and pay more for that added flexibility.

That approach does not force everyone into the smaller network. It gives employees a choice while helping the company manage cost. For some small businesses, that is a better strategy than choosing one plan and hoping it works for everyone.

#### A Simple Example

Imagine a 22-person technology company in Austin currently offers a broad PPO plan. The renewal increase is uncomfortable, and the employer is considering a lower-cost HMO. The owner worries employees will see the HMO as a downgrade.

Before deciding, the company asks employees to voluntarily list any providers they want to keep in network. The company does not ask why those providers matter.

After checking the HMO network, the advisor finds that most key providers are already included. A few are not. Now the owner has better information.

The company may offer the HMO as the low-cost base plan and keep the PPO as a buy-up option. Employees whose doctors are in the HMO can save money. Employees who want the broader network can pay more for it.

#### When This Strategy May Not Work

A doctor match review is useful, but it is not magic. It may not work well if employees are spread across many states, if the workforce has many dependents in different locations, or if provider access is already a sensitive issue. Some employees may also be uncomfortable sharing provider names, even without medical details.

Directories can also be imperfect. CMS has warned that provider directories are not always accurate, and prior CMS reviews of Medicare Advantage directories found significant inaccuracies. So the review should not be treated as a guarantee. It is a practical screening tool. The goal is to reduce avoidable disruption, not to promise that every provider relationship will stay unchanged forever.

#### The Bottom Line

A doctor match review is a simple way for small businesses to make better health insurance decisions. Instead of assuming a lower-cost HMO or focused network will not work, the employer can check whether employees’ important providers are already included. If the match is strong, the business may be able to move toward a lower-cost option with more confidence. If the match is weak, the employer can avoid a change that may create unnecessary frustration.

This is one area where small employers may have an advantage. They are often small enough to review provider fit in a practical, personal way. For small businesses, that can be the difference between simply accepting a higher premium and building a more thoughtful health insurance strategy.

#### FAQ SECTION

\1. What is a doctor match review?

A doctor match review compares the doctors, hospitals, specialists, and other providers employees want to keep using against the provider network of a health plan being considered.

\2. Can a doctor match review help lower health insurance costs?

It may. If many employees’ current providers are already included in a lower-cost HMO or focused network, the employer may be able to offer that plan without creating major disruption.

\3. Should employers ask employees for medical information?

No. Employers should avoid asking about diagnoses, prescriptions, conditions, or treatment history. The review should focus only on provider names and locations.

\4. Why do provider networks affect premiums?

Provider networks can affect what a carrier expects to pay for care. CMS rate-review instructions identify provider network and delivery system characteristics as plan-level factors in rate development.

\5. Are provider directories always accurate?

No. CMS says provider directories are not always accurate and recommends contacting the insurance company when network status is unclear.

\6. Can a small business offer both an HMO and a PPO?

In some fully insured small-group arrangements, an employer may be able to offer a lower-cost HMO or focused-network plan along with a broader PPO buy-up option. Carrier rules, contribution requirements, participation rules, and plan availability need to be reviewed.

\7. Is a lower-cost network always the right choice?

No. A lower-cost network only works if it gives employees reasonable access to the providers they need. The savings must be weighed against possible disruption.
