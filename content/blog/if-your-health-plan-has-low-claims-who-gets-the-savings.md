Imagine a small business has a surprisingly good health insurance year. Employees use the plan, but there are no major surgeries, no catastrophic hospital claims, and no unusually expensive ongoing treatments. The owner knows the company paid a large amount in premiums and assumes that a better-than-expected claims year should count for something.

Then the renewal arrives and the premium still goes up.

That experience frustrates many small business owners, especially those with younger or relatively healthy workforces. They naturally wonder: "If our claims were lower than expected, who benefited from that"?

The answer depends heavily on how the health plan is funded.

In a traditional fully insured small-group health plan, the employer generally does not receive a refund simply because its own employees had a low-claims year. Any Medical Loss Ratio rebate is based on the insurance carrier’s results across a broader state and market segment, not on one employer’s specific claims experience.

In some level-funded health plans, the employer may have an opportunity to receive a surplus refund if claims come in below the amount funded for claims, subject to the carrier’s contract terms and applicable rules.

That difference gets to the heart of a bigger question: Does your health plan structure allow your business to benefit when claims run well or, does that upside stay somewhere else?

## First, What Is the 80/20 Rule?

The Affordable Care Act created a rule known as the Medical Loss Ratio, or MLR, requirement.

In the individual and small-group health insurance markets, carriers generally must spend at least 80% of premium revenue on medical care and activities that improve healthcare quality. If they fall below that threshold, they may owe rebates.

This is often called the 80/20 rule. Simply put, most of the premium dollars should go toward healthcare and a smaller share can go toward running the insurance company. That sounds like a clear protection for consumers. And in some ways, it is. But the rule may not be working quite the way many people assumed it would.

## The 80/20 Rule May Not Reward Lower Costs the Way People Expect

The rule is based on a percentage of premiums, not on a fixed dollar limit. So if the total cost of healthcare rises, premiums usually rise too. And when premiums rise, the dollar amount left over for administration, business expenses, and profit also rises all while the insurer still follows the 80/20 rule.

But it does mean the rule may not create as much pressure to lower healthcare costs as many people assumed. If an insurer finds ways to reduce claims significantly, that may reduce the total premium base it can work from. And if too little of the premium dollar is spent on healthcare, the carrier may owe rebates.

Economists have raised this concern directly. A peer-reviewed study found that insurers affected by the Medical Loss Ratio rule increased claims spending after the rule took effect by about 7% in the individual market and 2% in the group market. The authors said the findings were consistent with a system that limits profit margins, but not necessarily total profit dollars.

In plain English: The rule was designed to stop insurers from keeping too much of each premium dollar. But it may not strongly reward the whole system for making healthcare less expensive overall.

## Meanwhile, Healthcare Costs Keep Rising

The 80/20 rule was created to make health insurance spending more consumer-friendly. But it did not stop healthcare costs from continuing to climb.

When the ACA was signed in 2010, total U.S. healthcare spending was about $2.6 trillion, or $8,402 per person. By 2024, national health spending had reached $5.3 trillion, or $15,474 per person.

Employer health insurance premiums rose sharply too. In 2010, the average annual premium for employer-sponsored family coverage was $13,770. By 2025, it had reached $26,993.

Those numbers do not prove that the 80/20 rule caused healthcare costs to rise. Many other forces have driven costs upward, including:

- Hospital prices
- Prescription drug costs
- New treatments and technologies
- Provider consolidation
- Increased use of medical services
- Chronic disease and high-cost conditions

But the numbers do help explain why a small business owner might fairly ask: "If the system was redesigned to protect premium dollars, why do premiums still keep rising and, who actually benefits when costs come in lower than expected?"

## The 80/20 Rule Is Not the Same as a Refund for Your Business

This is the part many employers do not realize.

If a fully insured carrier owes a rebate under the Medical Loss Ratio rule, the calculation is based on aggregated results across a state and market segment. It is not calculated based on whether your specific business had low claims. Federal rulemaking materials state clearly that rebates are not based on a particular group health plan’s experience or a particular subscriber’s experience.

In plain English:

- Your company can have an excellent claims year and receive no rebate.
- Another company can have a terrible claims year and still be part of a market segment where a rebate is issued.
- The rebate calculation is about the insurer’s broader results, not about whether your own business “beat expectations.”

This is a critical distinction for small business owners. A low-claims year in a fully insured small-group plan does not usually create a direct financial upside for that employer. The carrier has absorbed the risk of high claims, but it also generally retains the benefit of lower claims within the broader insurance pool, subject to Medical Loss Ratio rules.

## Why This Can Feel Misaligned for Some Small Businesses

Fully insured health insurance provides valuable certainty. The employer pays a fixed premium. If claims explode, the carrier bears that risk. That protection has real value. But the tradeoff is important: The employer pays for predictability, and in exchange, generally gives up the direct upside of a low-claims year.

For many small businesses, that tradeoff is perfectly acceptable. A company with volatile claims risk, an older workforce, or limited appetite for plan complexity may value the simplicity of fully insured coverage. But some employers look at that tradeoff differently.

A younger, relatively healthy business may think:

- “We are paying into a large premium pool.”
- “Our own claims are often lower than we expect.”
- “If there is favorable experience, we rarely see that come back to us directly.”
- “Is there a structure where we can share in that upside while still limiting downside risk?”

That question often leads to a discussion of level-funded health plans.

## How Level-Funded Plans Change the Conversation

A level-funded health plan is generally designed to feel predictable from a budgeting standpoint while making more of the underlying cost structure visible.

The employer typically pays a fixed monthly amount that covers:

- Expected claims funding
- Administrative costs
- Stop-loss protection against unusually high claims

If actual claims run lower than expected, some level-funded arrangements may offer a surplus refund at the end of the plan year, depending on the contract terms and applicable rules. That is a materially different employer experience.

In a fully insured model, when claims come in low, the employer usually does not receive a direct group-specific refund

In a level-funded model, when claims come in low, the employer may receive part of the favorable claims experience back through a contractual surplus refund

This does not mean level-funded plans are automatically better. They are not. Underwriting, eligibility, state availability, contract terms, stop-loss structure, claims volatility, and employer fit all matter.

But the “who benefits from a good claims year?” question is one of the clearest ways to explain why level funding can be compelling for the right small business.

## Are Level-Funded Refunds the Same as “Getting the Insurer’s Profit”?

Not exactly.

A level-funded refund is not literally the employer taking back “insurance company profit.” It is better understood as the employer potentially receiving part of the unused claims funding if the plan’s actual claims come in below the amount set aside for claims, subject to the plan’s contract.

Still, from the employer’s perspective, the practical contrast is real:

- In a fully insured plan, favorable claims experience generally benefits the broader insurance pool first.
- In a level-funded plan, favorable claims experience may be shared more directly with the employer.

That is an important strategic distinction.

## The Real Question Is Not “Are Insurers Bad?”

It is easy to turn this conversation into an attack on insurance companies. That is not the most useful path for a business owner.

The better question is: "Does my plan structure align the financial incentives the way I want them aligned?"

A fully insured carrier is paid to take the full claims risk and deliver a stable premium. In that model, the carrier needs to manage claims, comply with Medical Loss Ratio rules, and remain financially sound.

A level-funded arrangement still includes administrative fees and stop-loss protection, but it creates a more visible connection between the employer’s own claims experience and the employer’s potential financial outcome.

Neither approach is universally right. But owners should understand the difference.

## Why This Matters for Small Businesses

Many small businesses are squeezed between two realities: They need a credible health plan to recruit and retain employees but they cannot afford to treat every renewal as a blank check.

For employers with favorable demographics or relatively stable health experience, it is reasonable to ask whether a fully insured structure is still the best fit.

This is especially true if the business owner feels:

- “We keep paying more, but our own claims do not seem to explain it.”
- “The fully insured model gives us no visible upside in good years.”
- “We want to understand alternatives before accepting another renewal.”

That does not mean every small business should move to level funding. But it does mean the question is worth exploring.

## A Good Claims Year Should Start a Better Conversation

If your company has low claims, the first step is not to demand a refund from a fully insured carrier. That is usually not how the product is designed.

The better step is to ask:

- How does our current plan handle favorable claims experience?
- Are we in a structure where our own group’s performance matters financially?
- Would a level-funded comparison be appropriate?
- Do we value maximum predictability more than potential upside?
- Are we paying for a funding model that fits the company we have today?

Those questions lead to a much more useful benefits strategy discussion than simply chasing the lowest monthly premium.

## The Bottom Line

The ACA’s 80/20 Medical Loss Ratio rule was designed to make sure insurers spend most premium dollars on healthcare and quality-improvement activities. It created an important consumer protection.

But it may not reward lower total healthcare costs as strongly as many people assume. Because it is based on a percentage of premium, rising healthcare costs can still leave more total dollars available for administration, expenses, and profit while the carrier remains within the rule. Economists have raised that concern directly, and healthcare spending and employer premiums have continued rising sharply since the ACA became law.

For small business owners, though, the most practical takeaway is simpler: In a fully insured plan, your business usually does not directly receive the financial benefit of its own good claims year.

Medical Loss Ratio rebates are based on broader carrier results by state and market, not on one employer’s specific experience.

That is one reason level-funded plans deserve consideration for some small businesses. They may offer a more direct way to participate in favorable claims experience through a potential surplus refund, while still including protection against large claims.

The right plan is not the same for every employer. But every owner should understand: Who benefits when claims come in lower than expected?
