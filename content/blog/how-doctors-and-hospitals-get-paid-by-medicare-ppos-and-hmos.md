#### Why the Same Healthcare Service Can Cost Different Amounts in Different Health Insurance Networks

Most small business owners assume healthcare works like other purchases. A service has a price. The customer pays the price. The provider gets paid.

Health insurance does not work that simply. The same office visit, MRI, surgery, hospital stay, or outpatient procedure can be paid very differently depending on who is paying, which network is involved, and what contract exists between the provider and the payer.

That is one reason health insurance premiums can feel so confusing. The premium is not based only on how much care employees use. It is also affected by what the health plan has agreed to pay doctors, hospitals, clinics, labs, and other providers for that care.

This is where provider networks matter. A network is not just a list of doctors. It is also a set of payment relationships. A broad PPO network, a lower-cost HMO, Medicare, and an out-of-network arrangement may all pay differently for the same basic service. That does not mean anyone is doing something wrong. It means healthcare pricing is negotiated, layered, and often hard for employers to see.

For small businesses, understanding this can make health plan decisions much more practical. It helps explain why one plan may cost more than another, why HMOs may lower premiums, why PPOs offer more flexibility, and why network strategy belongs in every serious renewal review.

#### Medicare and Commercial Insurance Are Different Pricing Worlds

A good place to start is Medicare. Medicare is a government program with formal payment systems. It does not usually negotiate prices with every hospital and doctor in the same way a commercial insurance carrier does.

For many physician services, Medicare uses the Physician Fee Schedule. CMS explains that Medicare Physician Fee Schedule payments are based on relative value units, a conversion factor, and geographic adjustments. In simpler terms, Medicare uses a formula to decide what it will pay for many doctor and professional services.

For many inpatient hospital stays, Medicare uses the Inpatient Prospective Payment System, or IPPS. CMS explains that Medicare generally pays acute care hospitals a predetermined amount for each discharge, based on the average resources needed to treat similar cases, rather than paying separately for every individual item or service during the hospital stay.

That does not mean Medicare is simple. It is not. But the key point for a business owner is this: Medicare often pays through published formulas. Commercial insurance prices are usually based on negotiated contracts. That is a major difference. A hospital may receive one payment amount from Medicare, a different amount from one commercial PPO network, another amount from a different carrier’s HMO, and a very different amount from an out-of-network claim.

#### Commercial Insurance Often Pays More Than Medicare

Commercial employer health plans often pay providers more than Medicare does. RAND’s 2024 Hospital Price Transparency Study found that employers and private insurers paid hospitals an average of 254% of what Medicare would have paid for the same inpatient and outpatient services in 2022, with wide variation by state and hospital. That number is important, but it should be handled carefully. It does not prove that every hospital is overcharging. It does not prove that every insurer is failing to negotiate. It does not prove that Medicare always pays the “right” amount.

It simply shows that employer-sponsored commercial insurance operates in a different pricing environment than Medicare. For small business owners, the practical takeaway is simple: Your premium is affected not only by how often employees use healthcare, but also by what your plan pays when they do.

That is why network choice matters.

#### How Doctors May Be Paid

Doctors and other medical professionals can be paid in several ways. In many arrangements, doctors are paid fee-for-service. That means the doctor bills for a specific service, and the payer allows a contracted amount for that service. The payer may be Medicare, a commercial insurer, or another plan.

For example, if a doctor performs an office visit, the payment may be based on a fee schedule. Under Medicare, that fee schedule is formula-based. Under a commercial insurance contract, the allowed amount may be negotiated between the insurer and the provider group.

In some arrangements, doctors or medical groups may be paid through capitation. CMS defines capitation as a way of paying providers or organizations a predictable, upfront, set amount to cover the expected cost of all or some healthcare services for a patient over a period of time.

In plain English, fee-for-service pays when services happen. Capitation pays a set amount per patient over time.

Neither model is automatically good or bad. They create different incentives. Fee-for-service can reward volume because payment is tied to services provided. Capitation can reward prevention and cost control because the provider group receives a set amount and is responsible for managing care within that budget. But capitation also needs proper oversight, because a poorly designed system could create concerns about access or underuse of care.

This is why payment design matters. It shapes behavior.

#### How Hospitals May Be Paid

Hospitals can also be paid in different ways depending on the payer and contract.

Under Medicare’s inpatient hospital system, the payment is often tied to the type of case rather than every individual supply or service used during the hospital stay. CMS describes the IPPS as paying predetermined rates for each hospital discharge, with payment based on average national resource use for similar cases. Physicians are generally paid separately under the Physician Fee Schedule for their professional services during the inpatient stay.

Commercial insurance contracts can work differently.

A commercial insurer might pay a hospital based on negotiated rates for specific services. It might use case rates, per-diem rates, percentages of billed charges, outpatient fee schedules, or arrangements tied to Medicare rates. The exact contract terms are usually not visible to small employers.

That is part of the problem. The employer sees the premium. The employee sees the deductible or copay. But the underlying payment agreement between the insurance company and the hospital may be hidden from both.

That hidden pricing can affect claims. Claims affect premiums. So the contract still matters, even if the employer never sees it directly.

#### How a PPO Network Works

A PPO, or Preferred Provider Organization, usually gives employees more flexibility. Healthcare.gov explains that a PPO contracts with medical providers to create a network, and members pay less when they use providers in that network while still having the option to use out-of-network providers at an additional cost.

From the employee’s perspective, the advantage is choice. From the employer’s perspective, the tradeoff is cost.

A broad PPO may include many hospitals, doctor groups, and specialists. It may also offer out-of-network benefits. That can make employees more comfortable, especially if they are spread across multiple locations or have established doctors.

But that flexibility may mean the carrier has to include expensive providers in the network. If an important hospital system has strong negotiating power, the insurer may accept higher contracted rates to keep that provider in the network.

That is not sinister. It is negotiation.

The insurer wants a network employees will use. The hospital wants to be paid fairly, or as favorably as the market allows. The employer wants a premium that is affordable. The employee wants access. The final premium reflects that tug-of-war.

#### How an HMO Network Works

An HMO, or Health Maintenance Organization, usually has a more limited network than a broad PPO.

Healthcare.gov explains that an HMO usually limits coverage to care from doctors who work for or contract with the HMO, and generally will not cover out-of-network care except in emergencies. HMOs may also require members to live or work in the service area and often focus on integrated care, prevention, and wellness.

That structure can help lower costs in several ways.

First, the HMO may contract with a smaller group of providers. That gives the plan more ability to steer members toward certain doctors, clinics, hospitals, or medical groups.

Second, some HMO arrangements use more coordinated care. Employees may select or be assigned to a primary care doctor. Referrals may be required for some specialists. The plan may try to avoid unnecessary high-cost care by managing where and how care is delivered.

Third, some HMO or integrated-care arrangements may use payment models such as capitation. Instead of paying only when services happen, the plan may pay a provider group a set amount per member per month to manage some or all of that member’s care. CMS describes capitation as a set payment made upfront to cover expected healthcare services for a patient over a period of time.

This changes the financial incentives. A PPO often gives more freedom and pays through negotiated claims as services are used. An HMO may use a more controlled network and, in some arrangements, more predictable provider payment. That is one reason HMOs can sometimes produce lower premiums.

But there is a tradeoff. Employees may have less flexibility. Out-of-network care may not be covered except in emergencies. Some employees may dislike referrals or network limits. That is why an HMO should be evaluated based on actual provider fit, not just price.

#### Who Gets Paid in a Typical Claim?

A small business owner does not need to know every billing detail, but it helps to understand the basic flow.

The employer pays premium to the insurance carrier. Employees may also pay part of the premium through payroll deductions. When an employee receives care, the provider bills the insurance plan. The plan applies the contract, determines the allowed amount, subtracts the employee’s deductible, copay, or coinsurance, and pays the provider according to the plan rules.

The provider may receive money from both the insurer and the employee. The insurer keeps part of the premium for administration, claims processing, network contracting, reserves, taxes, and margin. The employer does not usually see the detailed contract rates behind each claim in a fully insured plan.

In a level-funded or self-funded arrangement, the employer may see more claims and cost data, depending on size, privacy rules, vendor reporting, and plan structure. But even then, the provider contracts are usually still controlled by the carrier, network, or third-party administrator.

The key point is this: The health plan is not just paying medical bills. It is applying a network contract. That is why the network matters.

#### Why Broad Networks May Cost More

Broad networks are popular because they reduce disruption. Employees can keep more doctors. They may have more hospital choices. They may have out-of-network options. This matters when a company has employees in multiple cities, employees with dependents, or employees who value provider flexibility.

But broad networks can also make it harder for the insurer to exclude high-cost providers. If employees expect access to certain hospital systems, the insurer may need those systems in the network to make the plan attractive. That can give major provider systems negotiating leverage.

Again, this is not about blame. It is normal market behavior. The provider wants strong payment. The insurer wants a usable network. The employer wants affordable premiums. The employee wants access. Broad networks often try to satisfy more of those demands at once, and that can raise cost.

#### Why Focused Networks and HMOs May Cost Less

A focused network or HMO may lower cost by being more selective. The plan may contract with a narrower group of hospitals, physician groups, and clinics. It may guide members toward lower-cost facilities or more coordinated care. It may reduce out-of-network exposure. It may use primary care more deliberately.

That can lower the plan’s expected claims. But lower cost only matters if the network still works for employees. If the HMO includes the doctors and hospitals employees already use, it may be a strong option. If it excludes important providers, employees may see it as a major downgrade.

That is why a doctor match review can be so useful for small employers. Before rejecting an HMO, the employer can check whether employees’ important providers are actually in the HMO network.

Sometimes the lower-cost network is more workable than people assume.

#### Why This Matters to Small Employers

Small employers usually cannot change how hospitals and insurance companies negotiate. But they can make better plan decisions when they understand the system.

A business owner reviewing a renewal should ask more than: “What is the premium?”

Better questions include: What network are we buying? Are employees using high-cost providers? Does a lower-cost HMO include the doctors employees care about? Are we paying extra for broad access that employees may not need? Could we offer a lower-cost base plan and a PPO buy-up option?

Those questions do not attack doctors, hospitals, or insurers. They simply recognize that networks are part of the pricing system. For a small business, that can turn a confusing renewal into a more thoughtful strategy discussion.

#### What This Does Not Mean

This article is not saying that doctors are paid too much, hospitals are bad, or insurers are hiding everything.

Healthcare pricing is complicated. Providers have real costs. Hospitals operate emergency departments, maintain expensive equipment, employ specialized staff and treat patients with different needs. Insurers build networks, process claims, manage contracts, and take financial risk.

The problem for employers is not that one side is always wrong. The problem is that the employer often sees only the premium, not the payment system behind it.

Understanding that system helps small businesses ask better questions.

#### The Bottom Line

The same healthcare service can be paid very differently depending on whether the payer is Medicare, a PPO network, an HMO network, or an out-of-network arrangement.

Medicare often pays using published formulas. Commercial insurers negotiate rates with doctors, hospitals, and provider systems. PPOs usually offer broader access and more flexibility. HMOs often use a more focused network and may rely more heavily on coordinated care or alternative provider payment models.

For small businesses, the practical lesson is simple: Provider networks are not just about which doctors employees can use. They are also about what the health plan pays for care.

That is why network choice can affect premiums, and why a strong renewal review should look behind the rate to understand the network, payment structure, and employee access tradeoffs.

The payment can vary depending on whether the payer is Medicare, a commercial PPO, an HMO, or an out-of-network arrangement. Each may use different payment rules, negotiated rates, or provider contracts.

A PPO usually pays in-network providers based on negotiated allowed amounts. Employees may also use out-of-network providers, usually at higher cost. An HMO usually limits coverage to providers that work for or contract with the HMO, except in emergencies. Some HMO or managed-care arrangements may use more coordinated care or payment models such as capitation.

\7. Does a lower-cost HMO always mean worse access?

No. A lower-cost HMO may work well if it includes the doctors, hospitals, and specialists employees already use. If important providers are missing, the savings may not be worth the disruption.

\8. What should small employers ask when comparing networks?

Small employers should ask which providers are included, how the network affects premium, whether key employee doctors are in network, and whether a lower-cost base plan plus a broader PPO buy-up option would make sense.
