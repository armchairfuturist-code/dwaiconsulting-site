Many small business owners are surprised to learn that employee health insurance pricing is not based entirely on the actual health of their own workforce.

In fact, under current ACA small-group rules, insurance carriers have far fewer ways to price plans than they did prior to the Affordable Care Act.

Before the ACA, small-group underwriting often considered factors such as:

- medical history,
- gender,
- industry,
- prescription usage,
- and prior claims experience.

Today, small-group pricing is generally much more standardized. In most ACA small-group markets, rates are primarily based on:

- age,
- geographic location,
- tobacco status,
- and family composition.

This change helped expand access to coverage and prevented employers from being heavily penalized for serious medical conditions within their workforce. However, it also changed how insurance costs are distributed across the market.

As a result, some employers believe their workforce demographics may cause them to subsidize higher-risk populations within the broader insurance pool.

For example, under ACA rules:

- men and women of the same age are generally charged the same premium,
- individuals with significant medical conditions typically pay the same as healthier individuals,
- and older employees cannot be charged more than three times the rate of younger employees.

This last point is particularly important.

Healthcare costs often increase significantly with age. In many cases, older individuals may use substantially more healthcare services than younger employees. However, because ACA rules limit age-based pricing differences, younger groups may effectively pay more relative to their expected claims costs in order to help stabilize pricing across the broader market.

To simplify the concept:

- a healthy 26-year-old employee may cost nearly as much as someone much older despite typically using fewer healthcare services,
- and a workforce with very few medical claims may still pay rates influenced by the broader community pool.

This does not mean the ACA is “bad” or that community rating lacks benefits. The system was designed to improve access and reduce pricing discrimination based on health status or medical history.

However, it does help explain why some small employers, particularly those with younger, healthier, or predominantly male workforces, sometimes begin exploring alternatives such as level-funded health plans.

Level-funded arrangements operate differently than traditional ACA community-rated plans. While they still include important protections such as stop-loss insurance, they may allow certain groups to have pricing that is more reflective of their own workforce characteristics and risk profile.

These plans are not appropriate for every employer, and they can involve additional underwriting requirements, participation rules, and eligibility considerations. Businesses with older populations, ongoing high claims activity, or certain medical conditions may not see the same advantages.

Still, for some employers, understanding how demographic factors interact with ACA rating rules provides valuable context when evaluating health insurance strategy.

Rather than viewing annual increases as random or unavoidable, businesses may benefit from taking a broader look at:

- workforce demographics,
- contribution strategies,
- participation levels,
- dependent enrollment,
- and alternative funding structures.

In some cases, that analysis may confirm that a traditional fully insured plan remains the best fit. In others, it may reveal opportunities worth further exploration.
